# Verbal Ability — Patterns, Methods, and Worked Questions

**Goal:** Choose the answer supported by grammar or passage evidence, not the answer that merely “sounds right.” The actual Infosys HRD test pattern has not been shared.

## Quick method by question type

| Type | How to solve | Frequent trap |
|---|---|---|
| Error spotting | Find the subject and main verb; then check tense, pronouns, modifiers, and parallelism | A long phrase between subject and verb hides disagreement |
| Fill in the blank | Predict meaning and part of speech before looking at options | Picking a familiar word that reverses the sentence's logic |
| Para jumble | Find introduction, pronoun links, chronology, and closing | Treating “this/they” as an opening without an antecedent |
| Reading comprehension | Map main point and paragraph roles; return to text for evidence | Importing outside knowledge |
| Inference | Pick what *must or is reasonably supported to* follow | Extreme language: always, never, all |
| Critical reasoning | Identify conclusion and its support; test the missing link | Mistaking a repeated premise for an assumption |

## 1. Subject–verb agreement

**Pattern:** “The list of shortlisted candidates **is** ready.” Head subject = *list* (singular); “of shortlisted candidates” is descriptive.

**Either/neither with or/nor:** “Neither the manager nor the employees **are** available”; “Neither the employees nor the manager **is** available.” In this standard construction, the verb agrees with the nearer subject. Recast awkward sentences when writing freely.

**Each/every:** “Each of the applicants **has** an ID.” *Each* is singular.

**Mini question:** “The results of the survey ___ useful.” Answer **are**; subject *results*.

## 2. Tense, sequence, and conditionals

**Pattern:** “By the time the interview began, she **had reviewed** the job description.” Earlier completed action takes past perfect when sequence needs clarity.

**Conditional:** “If I **had known** the deadline, I **would have submitted** the form earlier.” This describes an unreal past, not an actual submission.

**Mini question:** “She has worked on the dataset since Monday” versus “She worked on it last Monday.” *Since* can connect a continuing period to the present; a finished, specific past time usually takes simple past.

## 3. Pronoun reference, parallelism, and modifiers

**Ambiguous:** “When Riya spoke to Maya, she was upset.” Who was upset? Rewrite: “Riya was upset when she spoke to Maya.”

**Parallelism:** “The role involves **screening** resumes, **scheduling** interviews, and **maintaining** records.” Keep the grammatical form consistent.

**Dangling modifier:** “After reviewing the resumes, the shortlist was sent.” The shortlist did not review them. Better: “After reviewing the resumes, the recruiter sent the shortlist.”

**Mini question:** “The workshop focused on hiring, onboarding, and to retain employees.” Correct: “hiring, onboarding, and **retaining** employees.”

## 4. Articles, prepositions, and common usage

- “An **HR** manager” (spoken *aitch* begins with a vowel sound); “a **university**” (*yoo* begins with consonant sound).
- “Responsible **for** onboarding”; “interested **in** learning”; “comply **with** a policy.”
- “Fewer applicants” for countable people; “less time” for uncountable time.
- “Between two teams”; “among several teams” is a useful default, though usage depends on meaning.

**Mini question:** “She is responsible ___ employee records.” Answer **for**.

## 5. Vocabulary in context

**Pattern:** “The instructions were **ambiguous**, so students asked for clarification.” *Ambiguous* means open to more than one interpretation. Use the surrounding consequence to infer meaning.

**Contrast cue:** “Although the test was short, the questions were ___.” A word such as **challenging** fits contrast better than *easy*.

**Cause cue:** “The records were incomplete; therefore, the analysis was ___.” *Delayed* or *limited* may fit, depending on options. Read the full clause before deciding.

**Common pairs:** *affect* (usually verb) vs *effect* (usually noun); *principal* (main) vs *principle* (rule); *complement* (complete) vs *compliment* (praise).

## 6. Para jumbles — trace references

Arrange:

- **A.** This feedback helped the team revise the induction schedule.
- **B.** New joiners said the first week contained too much information.
- **C.** The revised schedule spread sessions across two weeks.
- **D.** The HR team asked new joiners about their induction experience.

**Solution:** **D → B → A → C.** D introduces the survey; B states feedback; “This feedback” in A refers to B; C states the revision. Do not place A first because *this* lacks a referent.

**Technique:** Test each pair, then read the proposed full sequence for time and logic.

## 7. Reading comprehension — text before intuition

**Passage:** “A company piloted short online learning modules for 80 employees. Attendance rose compared with the previous in-person series. The report did not measure whether employees applied the lessons at work.”

1. **What improved?** Attendance in the pilot, according to the report.
2. **Can we conclude the modules improved job performance?** **No**; application at work was not measured.
3. **Best summary?** “The pilot improved attendance, while its effect on workplace application remains unknown.”
4. **Inference:** The company would need a further measure to evaluate workplace application. This follows from the stated measurement gap.

**Trap:** An inference may also be **explicitly stated** in some tests; do not reject a true option merely because it repeats the passage. Reject unsupported additions and overclaims.

## 8. Main idea, tone, and purpose

**Pattern:** A passage presents benefits and limitations of hybrid training and recommends a pilot. Main idea = **balanced evaluation of a pilot**, not “hybrid training is always superior.”

**Tone:** Look for evaluative words. “May,” “could,” and “requires further evidence” suggest a **cautious/analytical** tone; they do not prove hostility.

**Purpose:** Inform, compare, persuade, criticise, or propose? Identify what the *whole* passage is doing.

## 9. Critical reasoning

**Argument:** “The online course had higher attendance than the classroom course; therefore it produced better learning.”

- **Conclusion:** Better learning.
- **Evidence:** Higher attendance.
- **Assumption:** Higher attendance is a reliable indicator of learning, or other learning evidence supports the conclusion.
- **Weakener:** A test of skill application shows no improvement after the online course.
- **Strengthener:** Comparable groups took the same post-training assessment and the online group performed better.

**Negation test for assumptions:** If “attendance has no reliable relationship with learning” could be true, the stated evidence no longer supports the conclusion.

## 10. Sentence completion and ordering under time pressure

1. Read the entire sentence/passage once.
2. Predict the logical role: contrast, cause, example, or conclusion.
3. Eliminate options with wrong grammar or meaning.
4. Read the chosen answer back into the full sentence.
5. In RC, locate a specific supporting line; in para jumbles, point to the linking word or reference.

## Try these before reading the key

1. “One of the interns ___ prepared the report.” (has/have)
2. “She is adept ___ analysing survey responses.” (at/in)
3. “The policy aims to reduce delays and ___ accuracy.” (improving/improve)
4. “Despite the short deadline, the team completed the task.” Is the tone surprised by completion or certain of failure?
5. Passage: “Thirty people attended; no follow-up assessment was done.” Can training effectiveness be established from attendance alone?

**Key:** 1 **has** (*one*); 2 **at**; 3 **improve** (parallel with *reduce*); 4 **surprised by completion**; 5 **no**, attendance does not establish effectiveness.

**Review habit:** For each error, record the exact rule or passage line that would have prevented it. Avoid memorising only the option letter.
