# Statements, Conclusions, and Assumptions

> **Difficulty:** Hard  ·  **Target time:** 90 s

## In simple words

**The idea:** An argument has evidence, a conclusion and an unspoken link between them. The unspoken link is the assumption.

**Think of it like:** A manager says 'send the reminder, some students forget'. It assumes a reminder will actually help.

**Simple steps:**
1. Underline the evidence and the conclusion.
2. Ask: what must be true for the evidence to support the conclusion?
3. Test it: if the assumption were false, would the argument collapse?

**Watch out:** Words like 'all', 'always' and 'never' make conclusions too strong and usually wrong.

## Method
Separate stated evidence, conclusion/recommendation, and unstated bridge. A conclusion must be supported; an assumption is needed for the argument to work. Negate a proposed assumption to test it.

## Recognise and solve
Statement: “Send a reminder because some students forget registration deadlines.”
- Recommendation: send reminder.
- Assumption: reminders can reach students in time and may help them act.
- Unsupported conclusion: every student will register.

Argument: “Attendance rose after online training, so learning improved.” Missing bridge: attendance indicates learning. A skill test showing no improvement weakens the argument.

## Tips and traps
Do not import outside facts. Words like “all,” “never,” and “only” often overstate evidence, but can be correct when premises truly support them. Negation must defeat the argument, not just be inconvenient.

## Try it
“Extend library hours because students need evening study space.” What assumption? **Answer:** extending hours can provide useful evening access to students.

## More worked patterns

### Pattern 1: Statement: “Take an umbrella; it may rain.” Is “rain is certain” an implicit assumption?

**Solution:** **No.** “May” expresses possibility. The advice assumes rain is possible and an umbrella could help.

### Pattern 2: Statement: “The company extended support hours to serve customers in other time zones.” What is the likely assumption?

**Solution:** At least some customers need support outside the old hours. This is a **necessary practical assumption** for the stated purpose.

### Pattern 3: Argument: “Two employees improved after training; therefore training improves everyone.” Identify the flaw.

**Solution:** The evidence is a two-person sample; the conclusion generalises to **everyone** without support. Other factors could explain the improvement.

## Deeper patterns (medium to hard)

### Extra rules and tricks
- Question types: assumption (needed unstated link), conclusion (follows from the statement), course of action (practical, directly related step), strengthen or weaken (evidence about the link).
- Course of action: choose steps that are feasible, effective and directly related to the problem; reject extreme steps like 'stop all hiring'.
- Assumption test: negate it; if the argument falls apart, it is a real assumption.
- Flaws to watch for: correlation taken as cause, selected examples, over-generalising from a few cases.

### Medium patterns

**M1. Statement: 'Use the lift; it saves time.' Is 'the lift is faster than the stairs' an assumption?**

**Solution:** **Yes.** The advice only makes sense if the lift is faster.

**M2. Statement: 'Company X has announced a hiring freeze due to rising costs.' (I) Costs are rising. (II) The company will fire staff.**

**Solution:** **Only I follows.** It is stated as the reason. II is not supported.

### Hard patterns

**H1. Problem: many employees leave within six months of joining. (I) Review the induction process. (II) Stop all hiring.**

**Solution:** **Only I follows.** It targets the cause. II is extreme and unrelated.

**H2. Argument: 'Employees who attend workshops are promoted faster, so workshops cause promotions.' Which fact weakens it?**

**Solution:** 'Workshop attendees were chosen because they were already top performers.' This gives **another explanation** for the promotions.
