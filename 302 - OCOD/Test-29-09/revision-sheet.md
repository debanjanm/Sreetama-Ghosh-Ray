## 1. Forces of OC (Unit 1)

### Definition & nature
- **Change (definition):** any alteration in the overall work environment of an organisation.
- **Nature of change:** results from internal + external pressure; disturbs existing equilibrium/status quo; affects the whole organisation (not just one part), at varying speed/significance across parts; can be reactive or proactive.
- **Reactive change** = brought about due to pressure of external forces.
- **Proactive change** = initiated by management on its own to increase effectiveness.
- 🧠 *Trick:* Reactive = **Re**acting to something outside; Proactive = **Pro**-active management choosing it first.

### Levels & types of change
- **Levels of change (3):** Individual, Group, Organisational.
- **Organisational-level change types (4):** Strategic, Structural, Process-Oriented, People-Oriented.
  - Strategic = mission/objectives change (e.g. BA/US Air culture merger example).
  - Structural = internal structure/reporting relationships.
  - Process-Oriented = tech, automation, robotics.
  - People-Oriented = performance, cohesion, self-actualization.
- 🧠 *Trick:* **S-S-P-P** — "**S**trategy **S**tructure **P**rocess **P**eople" — say it like a 4-beat chant. Strategic = the *why* (mission), Structural = the *shape* (org chart), Process = the *tools* (tech), People = the *heart* (morale).

### Change cycle — 8 steps
1. Develop new goals/objectives
2. Select an agent for change
3. Diagnose the problem
4. Select methodology
5. Develop a plan
6. Strategy for implementation
7. Implementation of the plan
8. Receive & evaluate feedback
- 🧠 *Trick:* Notice it's basically "**G**oal → **A**gent → **D**iagnose → **M**ethod → **P**lan → **S**trategy → **I**mplement → **F**eedback" — GADMPSIF. Read as a mini story: "you set a **G**oal, pick an **A**gent, **D**iagnose the issue, choose a **M**ethod, write a **P**lan, decide a **S**trategy, **I**mplement it, then get **F**eedback." The story order IS the answer to any "what comes before/after" question.

### External forces (4)
Technology, Marketing conditions, Social changes, Political forces.
- Technology: CAD (Computer-Aided Design), CIM (Computer Integrated Manufacturing), CNC, office automation.
- Marketing conditions: rapidly changing customer needs, tough competition.
- Social changes: equal opportunities to women, equal pay for equal work.
- Political forces: government interference, transnational corporations, world politics (reunification of Germany, Iraq's invasion of Kuwait, break-up of Soviet Union).
- 🧠 *Trick:* **T-M-S-P** = "**T**oo **M**any **S**illy **P**oliticians" (Technology, Marketing, Social, Political). Only 4 — if an option lists a 5th "force," it's really an internal one in disguise.

### Internal forces (6)
Nature of the workforce, Change in managerial personnel, Deficiencies in existing management structure, To avoid developing inertia, Human resource problems/prospects, Managerial behaviour/decisions.
- Nature of workforce: 50+ generation loyal to employer; mid-30s–40s loyal to themselves; youngest generation loyal to their careers; more women/dual-career couples.
- Deficiencies in mgmt structure: unmanageable span of management, too many managerial levels, lack of coordination, multiplicity of committees, lack of uniformity in policy, lack of cooperation between line & staff.
- HR problems/prospects: **Dissatisfaction is a symptom** of an underlying employee problem; absenteeism/turnover are also signals.
- 🧠 *Trick:* **W-M-D-A-H-M** = "**W**ise **M**ice **D**on't **A**lways **H**ide **M**istakes" (**W**orkforce, **M**anagerial personnel change, **D**eficiencies in structure, **A**void inertia, **H**R problems, **M**anagerial behaviour). 6 letters, 6 forces — count your fingers if unsure.

### Change agents
- **Change agent (definition):** person who initiates change in the organisation to increase organisational effectiveness.
- **Two types:** External change agents, Internal change agents.
  - External = behavioural scientists/consultants; take a **total, objective view** of the org as a system; not affected by internal norms; appointed for specific tasks (don't see change as ongoing); role = process consultation, diagnostic, comprehensive.
  - Internal = usually managers trained by external consultants; **accept the system as given**; apply change within existing norms; see change as an ongoing process; role is secondary, mainly implementation/education.
- 🧠 *Trick:* External = **outsider looking IN** (objective, one-time). Internal = **insider staying ON** (subjective, ongoing). "External = Eagle-eye view; Internal = In-house and continuing."
- **Skills required for a change agent:** communication, motivation/leadership, facilitation of group/individual activities, negotiation/influencing, planning & control, ability to manage upward/downward/peer, knowledge of the rationale for change.
- **6 roles of a change agent:** (1) announces the need for change (2) diagnoses the present situation (3) helps carry out change smoothly (4) formulates strategies for change — these are also called **OD interventions** (5) trains internal change agents (6) uses behavioural skills to handle emotional/social problems.

### Resistance to change
- Sources can be **Individual-level** or **Group/Organisational-level**.
- **4 individual-level factors:** Habit, Security, Selective Information Processing, Economic Factors.
  - Habit: hard to break routines (e.g. office relocation changing your commute/lunch routine).
  - Security: fear of losing job, role reorganisation.
  - Selective information processing: people "hear what they want to hear," ignore info that challenges their worldview.
  - Economic factors: fear of lower income (e.g. TQM example — workers fear they can't learn statistical process control).
- 🧠 *Trick:* **H-S-S-E** = "**H**appy **S**quirrels **S**ave **E**verything" (Habit, Security, Selective info, Economic).
- **2 group-level factors:** Group Inertia (disrupted informal norms), Structural Inertia (too much cohesiveness stifles change/adaptation).
- 🧠 *Trick:* Group Inertia = **norms** broken; Structural Inertia = **too-tight-knit** group resists. "Inertia" both times = resistance to moving at all, like Newton's First Law for teams.

### Strategies to implement change (5, general guidelines)
Use the Group Forces, Change of Change Agent, Share Rewards, Cooperation of Unions, Concern for Employees.
- 🧠 *Trick:* **U-C-S-C-C** = "**U**ncle **C**harlie **S**hares **C**ookies **C**arefully" (Use group forces, Change agent, Share rewards, Cooperation of unions, Concern for employees).

### Kotter & Schlesinger's 6 strategies for overcoming resistance (1979, *Harvard Business Review*, "Choosing Strategies for Change")
1. **Education & Communication** — best when resistance is due to lack of/inaccurate info; time-consuming if many people involved.
2. **Participation & Involvement** — best when initiators lack full info or others have power to resist; risk: time-consuming if participants design a bad change.
3. **Facilitation & Support** — best for adjustment problems; drawback: time-consuming, expensive, can still fail.
4. **Negotiation & Agreement** — best when a group will clearly lose out and has power to resist; drawback: can be too expensive, alerts others to negotiate too.
5. **Manipulation & Co-optation** — quick, cheap; drawback: can backfire badly if people feel manipulated (e.g. falsely claiming a plant will shut down unless workers accept a pay cut).
6. **Explicit & Implicit Coercion** — fast, overcomes any resistance; drawback: risky, leaves people angry (threats of transfer, demotion, layoffs).
- 🧠 *Trick:* **E-P-F-N-M-C** = "**E**very **P**arent **F**eels **N**ervous **M**eeting **C**oaches" (Education, Participation, Facilitation, Negotiation, Manipulation, Coercion). Notice the strategies get progressively **more forceful/less collaborative** as you go down the list — Education is gentlest, Coercion is harshest. If a question asks "least collaborative," think Coercion; "most collaborative," think Education/Participation.

## 2. Basics of OD (supplementary — not in the given PDFs)

- **OD (Organisation Development)** = a planned, systematic, organisation-wide effort, managed from the top, to increase an organisation's effectiveness and health through planned interventions using behavioural science knowledge.
- **Richard Beckhard's classic definition** — "an effort planned, organisation-wide, and managed from the top, to increase organisational effectiveness and health through planned interventions in the organisation's 'processes' using behavioural-science knowledge."
- **Key characteristics of OD:** planned change; organisation-wide/whole-system focus; managed from the top; long-term process; uses behavioural science; relies on Action Research; involves a change agent.
- **OD values:** respect for people, trust and support, power equalisation, collaboration, confronting issues openly, participation.
- OD is distinguished from ad-hoc/one-off change by being **continuous, data-driven, and collaborative**.

### OD history & key figures (safe, widely-established attributions)
- **Kurt Lewin** — father of Action Research and a founding figure of OD.
- **Richard Beckhard** — coined the classic textbook definition of OD.
- **Douglas McGregor** — also considered a founding figure of OD (alongside Lewin).
- **Robert Blake & Jane Mouton** — developed the **Managerial Grid**.
- **Chris Argyris** — known for the concept of **double-loop learning**.
- **National Training Laboratories (NTL)**, founded in the late 1940s, is linked to the T-group/laboratory-training movement that fed into OD's origins.
- 🧠 *Trick:* Don't confuse these OD-history names with the Models-of-OC names (Kilmann, Weisbord, Burke & Litwin, Trist/Bamforth/Emery) — those are a *different* set of people tied to *specific named models*, not to OD's founding history.

### OD intervention typology (Cummings & Worley)
4 broad categories: **Human process, Techno-structural, HR management, Strategic** interventions.
- 🧠 *Trick:* **H-T-H-S** = "**H**appy **T**urtles **H**ate **S**torms."

### OD consulting process
Typical sequence: **Entry & contracting → Diagnosis → Feedback → Joint action planning → Intervention/Action → Evaluation.**
- "Contracting" = clarifying expectations, roles, and terms of engagement up front — NOT a sales contract.
- 🧠 *Trick:* This is basically the SAME skeleton as Action Research below, just with an added "Entry/Contracting" step at the very front — OD's overall process *is* one big Action Research cycle repeated.

## 3. Action Research (supplementary — not in the given PDFs)

- **Pioneered by Kurt Lewin** — foundational methodology underlying most OD interventions.
- **Definition:** a data-based, problem-solving process mirroring the scientific method: gather data → feed back to client → jointly plan action → act → re-evaluate.
- **French & Bell's 8 steps, in order:**
  1. Problem identification
  2. Consultation with a behavioural science expert
  3. Data gathering & preliminary diagnosis
  4. Feedback to the client group
  5. Joint diagnosis of the problem
  6. Joint action planning
  7. Action
  8. Data gathering after action (→ cycle repeats)
- 🧠 *Trick:* Group them in pairs of "gather → respond": **(1) Problem → (2) Consult** an expert about it, **(3) Data-gather → (4) Feedback** it to the client, **(5) Joint-diagnose → (6) Joint-plan** together, **(7) Act → (8) Data-gather again** (which restarts the loop). Four pairs, each pair is "do something, then react to it."
- Core idea: it is **cyclical/iterative**, not a one-time fix — each action generates new data for the next diagnosis.
- Action Research is both a **research method** (generates knowledge) and a **change method** (produces actual improvement) at the same time.
- **Action Learning** (related but distinct) → associated with **Reg Revans**, not Lewin.
- **Participatory Action Research** → participants act as co-researchers throughout, not just subjects being studied.
- 🧠 *Trick:* If a question names **Reg Revans**, it's about **Action Learning**, NOT Action Research — easy trap since the names sound almost identical.

## 4. Models of OC (Unit 2)

### Kurt Lewin — Force Field Theory / 3-Step Model (**3 steps**)
Unfreeze → Change (Move) → Refreeze.
- Unfreeze = creating awareness of need for change (loosen the status quo).
- Change/Move = movement from old state to new state (implementation, takes time/investment).
- Refreeze = making the change permanent/resistant to reverting.
- The model helps a leader: (1) make a radical change (2) minimize disruption of operations (3) ensure the change is adopted **permanently**.
- 🧠 *Trick:* Think of an **ice cube**: melt it (Unfreeze), reshape it in a mould (Change), freeze it again in the new shape (Refreeze).

### Burke–Litwin Model (Warner Burke & George Litwin)
- Distinguishes **first-order change** (transactional/evolutionary/adaptive/incremental — climate-level) from **second-order change** (transformational/revolutionary/radical/discontinuous — culture-level).
- **Climate** = people's perceptions/attitudes (easy to change). **Culture** = deep-seated assumptions/values (hard to change).
- **Transactional leadership** → causes first-order (climate) change. **Transformational leadership** → causes second-order (culture) change.
- Diagram: **top half** = transformational factors (External Environment, Leadership, Mission & Strategy, Organizational Culture). **Bottom half** = transactional factors (Management Practices, Structure, Systems & Policies, Work Climate, Task Requirement, Motivation, Individual Needs & Values).
- Burke: two distinct sets of dynamics — one tied to everyday transactional exchanges (creates climate), the other to sudden "leaps" in behaviour needed for genuine culture change.
- 🧠 *Trick:* **Climate = weather today** (changes fast, superficial). **Culture = the climate zone/geography** (deep, takes years). Transactional leader = "let's make a fair trade today"; Transformational leader = "let's change who we fundamentally are."

### Ralph Kilmann — 5 Track Model (**5 tracks**)
Culture Track → Management Skills Track → Team-Building Track → Strategy-Structure Track → Reward System Track.
- Culture Track = starting point; 5-step process (surface actual norms → articulate what's needed → establish new norms → identify culture-gaps → close culture-gaps).
- Skills Track = teaches 5-step problem management (sense → define → derive solutions → implement → evaluate).
- Team Track = keeps dysfunctional behaviour in check, brings new learnings into daily work, enables cross-group cooperation.
- Strategy-Structure Track = **8-step** process (making strategic choices → objectives/tasks → analyse relationships → calculate inefficiencies → diagnose → design new structure → implement → evaluate).
- Reward System Track = **7-step** process (task force → review reward types → establish alternatives → debate assumptions → design new system → implement → evaluate); makes rewards vary directly with performance.
- 🧠 *Trick:* **C-S-T-S-R** = "**C**ats **S**it **T**ogether **S**haring **R**ewards" (Culture, Skills, Team, Strategy-Structure, Reward). Culture comes FIRST because "you can't fix skills/teams/structure/rewards until people trust each other enough to be honest" — that's *why* it's the starting track.

### Lippitt, Watson & Westley — 7-Phase Model (**7 phases**, extends Lewin's 3)
1. Diagnose the problem
2. Assess motivation & capacity for change
3. Diagnosing the client system's problem (clarification)
4. Establishing alternative routes
5. Transforming intentions into actual efforts
6. Stabilising change
7. Terminal relationship (exit)
- Maps onto Lewin: **Phases 1–2 = Unfreezing**, **Phases 3–5 = Moving/Change**, **Phases 6–7 = Refreezing**.
- 🧠 *Trick:* **D-A-C-E-T-S-T** = "**D**ogs **A**lways **C**hase **E**very **T**oy, **S**ometimes **T**wice" (Diagnose, Assess, Clarify, Establish, Transform, Stabilise, Terminate). Also remember the 1-2 / 3-5 / 6-7 split by counting on fingers: 2 + 3 + 2 = 7.

### Kotter's 8-Step Model (1996) (**8 steps**)
1. Create urgency
2. Form a powerful guiding coalition
3. Develop a vision & strategy
4. Communicate the vision
5. Remove obstacles / empower broad-based action
6. Create short-term wins
7. Consolidate gains
8. Anchor change in the corporate culture
- Advantages: easy step-by-step guidance; emphasises employee involvement/acceptability.
- Disadvantages: essentially **top-down**, discourages co-creation; skipping a step causes problems; time-consuming (Rose, 2002); can frustrate employees if individual needs are ignored.
- 🧠 *Trick:* **U-C-V-C-E-W-C-A** = "**U**rgent **C**ats **V**isit **C**omfy **E**mpty **W**arm **C**ozy **A**ttics" (Urgency, Coalition, Vision, Communicate, Empower/remove obstacles, Wins, Consolidate, Anchor). It's the ONLY 8-step model in this unit — if a question says "8 steps," it's Kotter (not to be confused with the 8-step *change cycle* from Forces of OC, or the 8-step Action Research from Topic 3 — three different "8"s in this exam!).

### Marvin Weisbord — Six-Box Model (**6 boxes**)
Purposes, Structure, Relationships, Rewards, Leadership, Helpful Mechanisms.
- Purposes: Do members agree with/support the mission & goals?
- Structure: Is there a fit between purpose and internal structure?
- Relationships: Quality of relations, modes of conflict, between people/departments.
- Rewards: What does the org formally reward, and for what?
- Leadership: Do leaders define/embody purposes? What's their normative style?
- Helpful Mechanisms: Do technologies/tools/processes help or hinder — these can become outdated.
- 🧠 *Trick:* **P-S-R-R-L-H** = "**P**lease **S**end **R**ich **R**elatives **L**ovely **H**ats" (Purposes, Structure, Relationships, Rewards, Leadership, Helpful Mechanisms). Note: Weisbord's box is just "**Rewards**," NOT "Reward System **Track**" — that longer name belongs to Kilmann. Classic trap.

### Sociotechnical Systems (STS) Theory
- Term coined by **Eric Trist, Ken Bamforth & Fred Emery** at the **Tavistock Institute** (London), based on a 1951 study of **English coal mines** — productivity fell and absenteeism rose despite better tech/pay, because the new production tech forced a rigid, bureaucratic structure onto workers.
- Core idea: **joint optimisation** of the social system AND the technical system together (not just one alone).
- Key concepts: **responsible autonomy** (self-regulating small groups with internal leadership), **adaptability**, **whole tasks** (one small group owns the entire task cycle), **minimal critical specification** (be precise about **what** must be done, not precise about **how**).
- **ETHICS methodology** (Mumford) = **E**ffective **T**echnical & **H**uman **I**mplementation of **C**omputer-based **S**ystems — uses Action Research to improve work design, involves employees in designing their own job systems.
- 🧠 *Trick:* "**S**ocio**T**echnical **S**ystems" = **STS**, and the founding trio's names all start differently but the place is easy: **T**avistock = **T**heory's home. Remember the coal-mine story as: "better machines, worse morale, because nobody asked the miners" — that's literally the whole point of joint optimisation.

### Organisational Design Models
Functional, Geographical, Product-Oriented, Market-Oriented, Matrix Structure.
- Functional = departments (Sales/Finance/HR/etc.), like a military chain of command — best for small/self-contained units.
- Geographical = divided by region (e.g. America/Europe/Asia) — local decision-makers suited to local culture (e.g. Russia = formal/distant boss vs USA = "team player" boss).
- Product-Oriented = divided by product line, each with its own team — used in entrepreneurial settings; can breed rivalry.
- Market-Oriented = divided by customer segment, not product — e.g. BMW/Ferrari/Ducati, niche customers, high personalisation.
- Matrix = employee reports to **two bosses at once** (functional supervisor + project/process manager) — used in construction/architecture/engineering; causes role conflict if the two bosses disagree.
- Conclusion: big multinationals often mix models — product-oriented in one country, matrix in another.
- 🧠 *Trick:* **F-G-P-M-M** — "**F**ive **G**iant **P**enguins **M**arch **M**errily." Matrix is the easiest to spot in a question: "reports to two bosses" = Matrix, every time.
