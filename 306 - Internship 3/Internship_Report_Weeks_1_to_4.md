# Internship Report: Weekly Learning Record

**Organisation:** Union Bank of India, Chennai Regional Office South  
**Internship focus:** Human Resource Management and Hybrid Learning & Training Effectiveness

## First Week – Understanding Organisational Structure and HR Functions

During the first week of the internship at Union Bank of India, Chennai Regional Office South, exposure was provided to the organisational structure of the bank and the functions carried out by the Regional Office. This initial orientation was important because a large public-sector bank functions through different levels of administration, with responsibilities distributed between the Head Office, Central Office, Circle Office, Zonal Office, Regional Office and branches.

It was understood that the Head Office, located in Mumbai, takes decisions on several centralised functions, including recruitment for clerical, probationary officer and specialist positions such as Agriculture, Information Technology, Marketing, Human Resources, Legal and Hindi Officer roles. Major policy matters relating to salary structure, employee benefits, disciplinary matters, industrial relations and broad training and learning-and-development policies are also handled at the central level. This ensures that important policies remain consistent across the organisation.

At the regional level, the Regional Office is responsible for administering the staff members working within the office and the branches allotted to that region. Chennai Regional Office South, located in Royapettah, was stated to have approximately 75 branches under its control. The Regional Office supports these branches in matters relating to employee welfare, attendance, leave, transfers within the region, reimbursement of work-related expenses, sanction of bills, salary-related processing and certain routine industrial-relations concerns.

The internship provided an understanding of the role of the Regional Office in translating central policies into day-to-day administrative practice. While the Head Office decides the overall salary structure and employee benefits, the Regional Office is involved in the processing of salary, permissible allowances, conveyance claims and bills. For example, when an employee travels from one branch to another for official work, the Regional Office may process the applicable travel and accommodation reimbursements according to bank rules.

Exposure was also provided to the leave-sanctioning process. It was explained that leave administration is carried out through defined procedures, depending on the type and duration of leave. Routine leave matters are generally handled at the Regional Office level, whereas longer periods of leave, such as maternity leave, sabbatical leave or leave for higher studies, require approval from the appropriate higher authority. As access to employee records is confidential, a temporary access ID was provided and an officer demonstrated, at a limited level, how leave is calculated and how salary is processed in relation to leave records.

Another important aspect of the first week was observing the daily communication process within the HR function. The day began with a brief Hindi prayer, followed by the reading of relevant circulars. These circulars helped employees remain updated on the performance of the bank, profits, non-performing assets, areas requiring improvement, policy instructions issued by the Head Office and mandatory training requirements. This practice showed the importance of regular internal communication in keeping employees informed about organisational priorities and responsibilities.

The internship also provided exposure to the changing nature of training and development in the banking sector. It was understood that learning is no longer limited only to conventional classroom training. Employees are increasingly required to participate in hybrid learning through live-streamed sessions, online modules and other digital formats, along with classroom-based programmes where required. Such learning opportunities are important because banking employees must continuously update themselves regarding products, technology, regulations, customer requirements and internal procedures.

Transfer policies and disciplinary procedures were also discussed during the orientation. It was understood that transfers between states are governed by broader policies and involve coordination between management, employee unions and the Head Office. However, transfers between branches within a particular region are generally administered by the concerned Regional Office. Clerical employees are required to have knowledge of the local language of the region in which they are posted. Officers may become eligible for transfer after completing the prescribed tenure, subject to their scale, level and service requirements.

With regard to misconduct, the internship provided a general understanding of the disciplinary process. When an issue is reported, the concerned authorities examine the matter and submit the required report through the appropriate channel. If necessary, further action is initiated through a formal inquiry process. Due to confidentiality, the exposure was limited to understanding the broad procedure rather than accessing individual cases or records.

### Learning and Key Takeaways

The first week provided a clear understanding that Human Resource Management in a public-sector bank operates across several administrative levels. While the Head Office frames major policies, the Regional Office plays an important role in implementing these policies and supporting employees and branches in their daily functioning.

A major learning was that HR functions in banking extend beyond recruitment and salary administration. They include employee welfare, leave management, transfers, training, internal communication, industrial relations and disciplinary procedures. These functions are closely connected with branch operations because employees require timely administrative support in order to perform their responsibilities effectively.

The exposure also highlighted the importance of confidentiality and professionalism. Banking organisations deal with sensitive employee, customer and financial information. Therefore, access to records is controlled, and employees as well as interns are expected to follow organisational procedures and maintain confidentiality.

Finally, the first week helped establish the connection between the internship and the dissertation topic on Hybrid Learning and Training Effectiveness. The daily reading of training-related circulars and the exposure to live-streamed and digital learning demonstrated that continuous employee development has become essential in the banking sector. Training is not an isolated activity; it helps employees adapt to changing systems, regulations and customer expectations while ensuring the continuity of banking services.

## Second Week – Exposure to Banking Operations and Functional Departments

During the second week of the internship at Union Bank of India, Chennai Regional Office South, exposure was provided to several important banking functions handled at the regional and branch levels. Since banking operations involve confidential information and are governed by regulatory requirements, the learning was mainly limited to understanding processes, responsibilities and functions. No sensitive employee, customer or financial data was accessed.

One of the functional areas introduced during this period was the Rural and Agriculture Business Department (RABD). This department is concerned with agricultural and rural banking activities, including agricultural lending, rural development initiatives, government-sponsored schemes and Priority Sector Lending (PSL). Through this exposure, it was understood that PSL requires banks to provide credit to identified sectors such as agriculture, micro, small and medium enterprises, education, housing and weaker sections of society. These sectors may otherwise have limited access to formal credit. Thus, rural and agricultural banking is not only a business function but also an important means of promoting financial inclusion and supporting economic development.

Exposure was also provided to the broad functioning of the Currency Chest and the importance of cash management and security in a bank. As currency operations are highly sensitive, no confidential cash-related information was made available. However, an understanding was gained of the wider processes relating to cash distribution, documentation, verification, security arrangements and regulatory compliance. Currency chests handle large volumes of cash; therefore, their functioning requires several layers of control, monitoring and accountability.

The internship also provided an understanding of the role of security functions in a banking organisation. Security arrangements include the deployment of security personnel, physical security of branches and currency chests, surveillance systems, CCTV coverage, alarm systems, fire-safety measures and emergency-response procedures. Such arrangements are necessary to protect employees, customers, cash, records and other valuable assets of the bank. This exposure showed that security is not a separate activity; it supports the continuity and reliability of daily banking operations.

Overall, the second week made it clear that banking is not restricted to customer-facing services alone. A bank functions through several interconnected areas, including agricultural and priority-sector lending, cash management, security, compliance and risk control. Each function has a direct or indirect effect on the quality, safety and accessibility of banking services.

### Learning and Key Takeaways

The major learning from this week was that public-sector banking has both commercial and social responsibilities. Through RABD and Priority Sector Lending, the bank contributes to rural development, financial inclusion and access to formal credit for sections that may not otherwise receive adequate financial support.

Another important takeaway was the role of confidentiality and compliance. Banking employees handle sensitive information and valuable assets; therefore, access to information is regulated according to the nature of work and responsibility. This helped in understanding why interns and employees must follow professional boundaries, maintain confidentiality and respect organisational procedures.

Finally, the exposure highlighted that functions such as security, cash management and compliance are essential for customer confidence. Although these functions are often less visible than branch counters or customer services, they enable the bank to operate safely, reliably and in accordance with regulatory requirements.

## Third Week – Branch Visits and Primary Data Collection

During the third week of the internship at Union Bank of India, Chennai Regional Office South, the opportunity was provided to move beyond the Regional Office and visit different branches under the region as part of the dissertation research on Hybrid Learning and Training Effectiveness. The purpose of these visits was to understand how employees experience training within their actual work environment and how classroom-based, digital and live-streamed learning are perceived by officers and clerical staff.

As the research involved the collection of primary data, several branches were visited and employees were approached during their working hours. The branches covered included Triplicane 1, Triplicane 2, Triplicane 3, Alandur, Teynampet, Mowbrays Road, Chamiers Road, Mylapore 1, Mylapore 2, Mylapore 3, Adyar, Madhya Kailash, Besant Nagar 1, Besant Nagar 2, Indiranagar, Shastri Nagar, T. Nagar 1, T. Nagar 2, T. Nagar 3, West Mambalam, Nungambakkam, Koyambedu, Virugambakkam, Saligramam, Ashok Nagar 1, Ashok Nagar 2 and Ashok Nagar 3. Responses were also collected from employees in Regional Office departments, including Retail Loans and Currency Chest.

These branch visits made it possible to observe that the work environment, staffing pattern and customer footfall can vary substantially from one branch to another. Some branches had a larger number of employees and higher customer interaction, while some were functioning with comparatively limited staff strength. For example, during the visit to Triplicane 2, there were only a few staff members, including two officers and one cashier. Similarly, Teynampet, despite being a comparatively large branch, had a limited number of officers available at the time of the visit.

The visits were carried out during active working hours. Therefore, it became clear that employees must attend to customers, transactions, documentation, loan-related work, cash operations and other branch responsibilities while also completing mandatory learning requirements. In this context, training cannot be examined only as a separate HR activity. Its timing, accessibility and relevance must be considered alongside the operational workload of the branch.

Interactions were held with both officers and clerical staff to understand their experiences of training, the methods through which they had received training earlier, and the changes they had observed over time. The discussions focused on classroom programmes, online learning, live sessions, digital learning modules and other blended forms of learning. The purpose was not to collect confidential information, but to understand employees’ general perceptions of training effectiveness and their ability to apply learning in day-to-day work.

At Alandur, located at Arumbakkam, an interaction was held with an experienced manager who had previously served in the armed forces. This discussion highlighted that employees are continuously expected to update themselves in response to changing banking practices, products, technology and customer requirements. It also showed that training is not limited to technical banking knowledge. It may include customer service, communication and the ability to deal with customers from different backgrounds and with different requirements.

The research questionnaire was administered in accordance with the variables of the dissertation. It did not require participants to disclose customer information, account details or sensitive internal data. As the research was conducted in a public-sector bank, confidentiality was maintained throughout the data-collection process. Employees were approached respectfully, and the interaction was confined to their general training experiences, opinions and perceptions.

### Learning and Key Takeaways

The third week provided a practical understanding of the difference between studying training in theory and observing it in an actual banking environment. Training models may appear straightforward in textbooks; however, in branches, customer footfall, manpower availability, work pressure and time constraints directly influence how employees participate in learning programmes.

It was also understood that flexibility is important in banking training. Digital learning may help employees access content without travelling away from their work location, whereas classroom or live training remains valuable when direct interaction, discussion and clarification are necessary. Therefore, a balanced approach may be more suitable than relying only on one mode of training.

Most importantly, the branch visits developed an understanding of professional communication, research ethics and employee engagement. The experience of approaching staff members during busy working hours required sensitivity towards their responsibilities. It also reinforced that research in an organisational setting must respect confidentiality, time constraints and the voluntary participation of respondents.

## Fourth Week – Training Practices, Organisational Change and Research Learning

During the fourth week of the internship, branch visits and research-related interactions were continued in order to obtain a broader understanding of training practices in Union Bank of India. A major area explored during this period was the experience of employees following the amalgamation of Andhra Bank and Corporation Bank with Union Bank of India. The purpose was to understand, at a general and non-confidential level, how employees adapted to changes in systems, processes, organisational practices and training requirements.

Before the amalgamation, Andhra Bank, Corporation Bank and Union Bank of India functioned as separate institutions. Employees who had worked in Andhra Bank and Corporation Bank were familiar with their respective organisational procedures, workflow patterns, systems and training practices. Following the integration, employees had to adapt to a common organisational structure and the processes followed by the combined bank. This required continuous communication, learning and support because employees had to become familiar with updated systems, technology, products, reporting requirements and work practices.

As part of the research interaction, officers and clerical staff were asked about the training methods they had experienced before and after the amalgamation. The focus was on their general experience of learning rather than on confidential internal instructions or documents. Employees shared their perceptions of earlier training practices, classroom programmes, digital modules, online sessions and the support required to adapt to new ways of working. These interactions helped in understanding how training can support employees during a major organisational transition.

The discussions also provided a broad understanding of Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) in banking. SOPs provide employees with a structured way of carrying out routine work, maintaining documentation, following authorisation levels, ensuring compliance and serving customers consistently. Before the amalgamation, employees may have been accustomed to the procedures and systems of their respective legacy banks. After integration, they needed to understand the common processes and revised practices followed by the merged organisation. This adjustment was important because branch operations require accuracy, uniformity and adherence to regulatory requirements.

The experience showed that a merger of this nature cannot be managed through one-time orientation alone. When employees from different organisational backgrounds come together, they require continuing updates and opportunities to clarify doubts. New processes may affect daily activities such as customer service, documentation, transactions, lending-related work, digital banking support and internal reporting. Therefore, training becomes an important part of change management, as it helps employees understand the reason for change and apply revised procedures with greater confidence.

The internship also reinforced the relevance of hybrid learning in a public-sector banking context. On the one hand, digital modules, online sessions and live-streamed programmes can provide flexibility, especially when employees cannot leave a branch because of workload or customer footfall. On the other hand, classroom and interactive sessions remain useful when employees require direct guidance, discussion, practical demonstration and clarification. A combination of both methods can help employees access learning while allowing branches to continue their customer-facing operations.

Another important observation was that training effectiveness depends not only on the mode through which content is delivered, but also on whether employees are able to retain and apply the learning in their actual work. Employees may attend a programme or complete an online module, but the learning becomes meaningful only when it helps them perform their responsibilities more confidently, follow updated procedures and respond effectively to customer needs.

### Major Learnings and Key Takeaways

The third and fourth weeks were particularly significant because they connected academic research with workplace reality. The internship demonstrated that employee training in banking must be continuous. Employees cannot depend only on the knowledge acquired when they initially join the organisation because banking regulations, technology, products, systems and customer expectations continue to change.

A second major learning was the practical value of hybrid learning. Digital learning can reduce travel and scheduling difficulties and may enable employees to access training more flexibly. At the same time, classroom and interactive learning are important where practical explanation, direct communication and immediate clarification are required. The effectiveness of a hybrid approach depends on the training purpose, employee workload and the support available to apply learning at work.

The research also showed that staff availability and workload can affect training participation. Branch employees work in a customer-facing environment, where transactions, documentation, customer queries and operational responsibilities require constant attention. Therefore, training schedules should be planned in a manner that supports employee development without unnecessarily affecting branch operations or customer service.

The merger-related discussions provided an important learning on change management. When different organisations are integrated, employees need clear communication, continuous learning and adequate support to adapt to common systems and procedures. Training can reduce uncertainty and help employees understand how organisational changes affect their own work responsibilities.

Finally, the overall internship strengthened the understanding that HR functions in a banking organisation are closely linked with business operations. Training and development are not isolated HR activities. They help employees adapt to change, update their knowledge, follow procedures, use technology and provide better service to customers. The branch visits and data-collection process also developed practical skills in research ethics, professional communication, confidentiality and respectful interaction with employees in a formal work setting.
