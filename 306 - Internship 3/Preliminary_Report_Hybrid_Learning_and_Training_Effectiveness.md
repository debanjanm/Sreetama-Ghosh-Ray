## 1. Purpose of the Report

This preliminary report summarises the fieldwork undertaken during the internship at Union Bank of India, Regional Office Chennai South, in connection with the dissertation on **Hybrid Learning and Training Effectiveness**. The study examines how employees experience different modes of training in a public-sector banking environment, particularly classroom-based programmes, online modules and live-streamed sessions.

The purpose of the report is not to evaluate individual employees, branches or internal policies. It is intended to present the practical context of the research, the broad approach followed during data collection and the preliminary learning derived from the field exposure. Detailed statistical analysis, hypothesis testing and academic interpretation will be presented separately in the final dissertation after the research data have been compiled and analysed.

## 2. Background and Rationale

Banking work is changing continuously because of digitalisation, new products, revised procedures, regulatory requirements and changing customer expectations. Employees therefore require regular opportunities to update their knowledge and skills. At the same time, training has to be organised around the practical realities of branch banking, where employees are responsible for customer service, transactions, documentation, lending-related work, compliance and other time-sensitive activities.

In this context, hybrid learning has become relevant. A hybrid approach combines more than one method of learning, such as classroom training, online learning modules, live virtual sessions and work-based application. Online and live-streamed sessions can improve access and reduce travel or scheduling difficulties. However, classroom and interactive sessions remain important where employees require direct guidance, discussion, demonstration or immediate clarification. Therefore, the effectiveness of training cannot be understood only from the delivery mode; it also depends on whether employees are able to understand, retain and apply the learning in their day-to-day work.

The internship provided an opportunity to study this issue in a real organisational setting. It also helped connect the dissertation topic with the wider Human Resource Management function of a public-sector bank, where training supports employee capability, process consistency, customer service and organisational change.

## 3. Objectives of the Study

The study is guided by the following objectives:

1. To understand employees’ broad exposure to classroom, online and live-streamed training methods.
2. To examine employees’ perceptions of the usefulness and practical applicability of different training modes.
3. To understand how workload, staffing availability, customer footfall and branch responsibilities may affect participation in training.
4. To explore the role of training in helping employees adapt to changes in systems, procedures and organisational practices after amalgamation.
5. To identify practical considerations that may support training effectiveness in a public-sector banking context.

## 4. Internship and Fieldwork Context

The internship began with orientation to the organisational structure and HR functions of Union Bank of India. The exposure covered the relationship between Head Office, Zonal Office, Regional Office and branches, as well as the role of the Regional Office in areas such as employee welfare, attendance, leave administration, transfers, salary and allowance processing, training communication and industrial relations.

This orientation was important for the research because training is closely linked with these operational and HR functions. Learning-related circulars, mandatory programmes and staff-development requirements have to be communicated to employees while branch services continue without interruption. The fieldwork also provided broad exposure to the Rural and Agriculture Business Department, Priority Sector Lending, Currency Chest operations and bank-security arrangements. These areas reinforced the importance of continuous learning, accuracy, compliance and confidentiality in banking work.

The data collection was undertaken during normal working hours at the Regional Office and across the branch network. Employees were approached respectfully, keeping in mind their customer-facing and operational responsibilities. The interaction was limited to general training experiences and perceptions. No customer information, account details, employee records, confidential operational documents or sensitive internal data were sought, accessed or recorded.

## 5. Coverage and Method of Data Collection

Primary data were collected through a structured questionnaire on hybrid learning and training effectiveness, supported by brief professional interactions with officers and clerical staff. The questionnaire focused on employees’ general experience of different learning formats, their views on accessibility and usefulness, and the extent to which training could be applied in work situations.

The fieldwork covered 27 branches under the Chennai South region, along with interactions in Regional Office departments, including Retail Loans and Currency Chest. The branches approached were:

- Mylapore 1, Mylapore 2 and Mylapore 3
- Adyar, Madhya Kailash, Besant Nagar 1 and Besant Nagar 2
- Indiranagar and Shastri Nagar
- T. Nagar 1, T. Nagar 2 and T. Nagar 3
- West Mambalam, Nungambakkam, Koyambedu, Virugambakkam and Saligramam

The study is based on voluntary responses and field interaction within the available internship period. It should therefore be read as an academic study of employee perceptions and operational context, rather than as an audit, compliance review or performance assessment of the bank.

## 6. Preliminary Observations from the Field Context

The field exposure showed that branch-level work conditions differ across locations. Staffing availability, customer footfall, operational workload and the nature of branch activities may affect the time employees can set aside for training. During busy hours, employees must prioritise customer requirements, transactions, documentation, cash-related work, service requests and internal responsibilities. Thus, training participation has to be considered alongside the practical requirement of maintaining service continuity.

Employees were familiar with more than one form of learning. Classroom programmes were viewed as useful where direct interaction, explanation and discussion were required. They can be particularly valuable when employees need to understand a new process, clarify doubts or practise application with guidance. At the same time, online modules and live-streamed sessions offer flexibility, especially where travel to a training centre may be difficult or where employees cannot be away from the branch for a long period.

The central issue is not that one method replaces another. A wholly digital model may provide access and flexibility, whereas a wholly classroom-based model may provide stronger interaction but involve time and travel constraints. In a public-sector banking environment, a balanced combination of methods may be more workable when it is matched to the learning objective, branch workload and the support available to employees after the programme.

Another relevant area was the experience of organisational change after the amalgamation of Andhra Bank and Corporation Bank with Union Bank of India. Employees from the legacy banks had previously worked with their respective systems and established procedures. After the integration, employees needed to become familiar with common processes, revised systems, documentation requirements, authorisation practices and work routines. The field interaction explored training experiences related to this transition at a general level, without reviewing confidential internal instructions or individual cases.

This context highlights the importance of training as a part of change management. A one-time orientation may introduce a new process, but continuing opportunities for learning, clarification and application can help employees adjust with greater confidence. This is particularly significant in banking because employees need to apply updated knowledge carefully and consistently while serving customers and meeting compliance requirements.

## 7. Relevance to Training Effectiveness

The dissertation considers training effectiveness not merely as attendance at a programme or completion of an online module. Training becomes effective when employees can understand the content, retain the learning and use it appropriately in their work. In a banking setting, this may include improved confidence in handling procedures, greater familiarity with products and systems, more consistent documentation, better customer interaction and the ability to respond to updated requirements.

Several practical conditions may influence this process:

- **Relevance of content:** Training is likely to be more meaningful when employees can connect it directly with their role and work responsibilities.
- **Access and timing:** Flexible modes can support participation, particularly when employees have limited time to leave their branch or department.
- **Interaction and clarification:** Live and classroom sessions remain important when the subject requires discussion, demonstration or immediate doubt-clearing.
- **Managerial and organisational support:** Employees require reasonable time, access to learning platforms and encouragement to complete and apply training.
- **Follow-up and application:** Reinforcement after the programme can help translate learning into regular work practice.

These are preliminary field-based considerations. The final dissertation will examine the collected data systematically before drawing empirical conclusions.

## 8. Preliminary Suggestions for Consideration

Based on the field context, the following suggestions may be considered as broad, non-binding inputs for training planning:

1. **Use a blended approach according to the training objective.** Digital modules may be suitable for standard updates and self-paced learning, while interactive classroom or live sessions may be more suitable for new procedures, complex topics and application-based learning.
2. **Plan training around branch operating conditions.** Training schedules may be more workable when they consider customer footfall, staff availability and peak operational periods.
3. **Provide scope for clarification after digital learning.** Short live question-and-answer sessions, practical demonstrations or access to a resource person may help employees apply learning more confidently.
4. **Link training with post-amalgamation adaptation.** Continued learning support can help employees understand common processes and work consistently across the organisation.
5. **Encourage work-based reinforcement.** Job aids, concise reference material and supervisor support can help employees use the learning after completion of a programme.

These suggestions are offered with the understanding that training decisions are governed by the bank’s established policies, operational requirements and learning-and-development systems.

## 9. Learning Derived from the Internship

The internship established that training and development are not isolated HR activities. In a public-sector bank, they are connected with technology, operational accuracy, compliance, customer service and organisational change. The experience also showed that the success of a learning programme depends on the organisational environment in which employees are expected to use it.

The branch visits strengthened practical skills in professional communication, confidentiality and research ethics. Approaching employees during active working hours required sensitivity to their duties and time constraints. It also reinforced the importance of collecting research data responsibly and presenting findings without disclosing individual or confidential organisational information.

Most importantly, the fieldwork gave the dissertation a practical foundation. It moved the study of hybrid learning beyond a theoretical comparison of classroom and digital methods and situated it within the daily realities of public-sector banking.

## 10. Conclusion

The preliminary fieldwork indicates that hybrid learning is a relevant area of study for public-sector banks. Employees require continuous skill upgradation, but the mode and timing of learning need to fit the operational realities of the workplace. Digital and live-streamed learning can improve access and flexibility, while classroom learning continues to have value where direct interaction and practical explanation are required.

Therefore, the most useful approach is likely to be one that combines methods thoughtfully, rather than treating any one format as sufficient for every training need. The final dissertation will analyse the questionnaire responses in detail and present evidence-based conclusions on hybrid learning and training effectiveness.
