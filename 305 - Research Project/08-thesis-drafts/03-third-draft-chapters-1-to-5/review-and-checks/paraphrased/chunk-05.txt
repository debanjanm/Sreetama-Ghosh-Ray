Holton (1996) suggests that the different levels of evaluation alone cannot fully explain how findings are applied in real-world settings. Baldwin and Ford (1988) explain that transfer occurs when learning is generalized and maintained in the workplace. Blume and their team in 2010 discovered connections between the process of transferring knowledge and various factors, including the learner's personal traits, how the training was structured, and the conditions of the workplace. These sources warn that the effectiveness of later applications is not solely based on the training event itself.
The result being measured here is more specific: employees assess how well they understand the content, find it relevant, feel capable of using it, see its use in their work, get help with related issues, and feel confident in their current roles. They are not required to estimate productivity, customer satisfaction, reputation, or financial results for the Bank, as this would need additional data.
Aziz's (2015) General Training Effectiveness Scale was created specifically for use in workplace learning within Malaysia. The difference between individual learning and organisational results guides the current questionnaire's focus on employees. The wording from Union Bank was checked for content, tested in a trial, and evaluated using reliability analysis.
Bahl, Kiran and Sharma (2024) examine a wider chain that encompasses the outcomes. This survey does not have its own records regarding the productivity of branches, the results for customers, or the patterns of behavior seen over time. It could also ask if employees understood the material, realized its importance, and felt confident in applying it. That is an outcome observed at the employee level, rather than a complete Kirkpatrick evaluation.
Aziz (2015) also ensures that individual learning remains separate from the achievements of an organization. A person cannot fairly assess the overall outcome of the bank based on just one recent program. The job performance was not included in the model for a related reason: actual performance requires a suitable measure and evidence that goes beyond the same employee's account of training.
Workplace Implementation Conditions
An LMS can store content, but individuals still have to determine which materials should be reviewed prior to a session, what topics require discussion, and when feedback is necessary. A classroom discussion might not provide employees with materials that they can review again later. This link between activities is key to the Hybrid Learning measure.
Mubayrik's 2018 review of workplace blended learning highlights several common themes, including flexible access, real-world activities, teamwork, learner satisfaction, and technological support. The studies differ in terms of the population they involve, the way they are structured, and how they measure outcomes. The diversity of options is a key factor in considering the task, trainer, technology, and the chance to take part in a specific environment, rather than assuming that a single method of delivery is equally effective in all situations.
The order of tasks is important when employees need to allocate time for training while handling their regular work responsibilities. A digital activity may present a policy or example prior to a live session that focuses on cases and practical applications. A trainer could start by teaching a challenging subject and then provide digital resources for review later. The questionnaire asks if the selected program's activities seemed connected and easy to reach; it does not list or order these possible sequences.
One option is to prepare digitally, practice with a trainer, apply skills in the workplace, and review using digital tools. This example shows how a connected program could appear. It is not another model that was tested in the survey.
Use of the Literature in Practical Recommendations
Recommendations should start by presenting the actual ratings given by respondents, followed by an analysis of those ratings using existing literature. Low access or material scores suggest issues with the timing and availability of resources. Lower scores in terms of explanation or feedback may indicate that the trainer needs to review their preparation. Limited involvement or asking for more information would focus on questions, practice, and follow-up.
Employee ratings highlight where a program is strong or where it might be challenging; the reviewed studies explain why these areas are important.
Summary of the Empirical Review
The sources perform complementary roles. Sosnova et al.(2025) link hybrid methods with learning effectiveness in education; Kim (2022) examines corporate training design, instructors, practice, and outcomes; and Bahl, Kiran, and Sharma (2024) assess training among Indian bank employees. Han and Ellis (2020), Mulaudzi (2021), and Aziz (2015) provide the themes and language used in the questionnaire related to the workplace.
No single source has the full model or a ready-made instrument for Union Bank. Appendix B explains how the scored items are based on these different areas of literature.
Comparison of the Three Base Papers
The foundational papers examine three different scenarios instead of focusing on a single, identical model. Sosnova et al.(2025) conducted their research with students; Kim (2022) focused on automotive sales trainees; and Bahl, Kiran, and Sharma (2024) studied Indian bank employees, but none of these studies included a hybrid-delivery approach. Their differences cause the Union Bank's question to be significant, but neither provides a result that can be directly copied in Chennai.
Their methods also set limits on comparison. Sosnova and Kim examine different arrangements or results under various delivery conditions, whereas Bahl and their team apply a multi-level approach to evaluate outcomes. The existing single-use survey includes four ratings and evaluates one regression. It cannot demonstrate that altering Union Bank’s delivery method would recreate a previous outcome or achieve results at the organisational level.
Kim's conversation about instructors and participation, Mulaudzi's (2021) account of facilitation and time in the workplace, and Schaufeli, Bakker, and Salanova's (2006) idea of active effort all contribute to distinguishing between the support provided by trainers and the actions taken by learners. The final source relates to overall work engagement, which is used as background information instead of a specific tool for training. A bank worker might find a trainer's explanation helpful but may not have enough time to get ready or ask questions. The two experiences are therefore measured separately.
The sources used for measurement serve different purposes. Han and Ellis (2020) raise questions regarding the relationship between modes, Mulaudzi (2011) discusses the conditions related to workplace access, and Aziz (2015) focuses on outcomes at the employee level. Appendix B documents how those themes were applied to a specific Union Bank program. Neither a student-scale tool nor a transport-sector instrument can become a validated Bank measure just by altering its wording.
The literature points to a single focused test: among employees who experienced both methods, do more positive evaluations of integration, trainer skill, and involvement relate to a higher sense of training effectiveness? The response can highlight areas that need to be checked, but the single design does not address the order of events or cause and effect.
