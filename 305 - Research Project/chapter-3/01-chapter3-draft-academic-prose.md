## 3.1 Objectives of the Study

This study's objectives follow directly from the conceptual model developed in Chapter 2: one primary objective testing the model's central mechanism, and five specific objectives addressing its individual components and downstream outcomes.

**Primary objective.** To examine the influence of hybrid learning on training effectiveness among employees of Union Bank of India, through the mechanism of learner engagement.

**Specific objectives.**

1. To examine the impact of blended learning on employee knowledge retention.
2. To evaluate the effect of blended learning on workplace performance.
3. To compare the effectiveness of digital-only, classroom-only, and blended training approaches.
4. To examine learner engagement as the mechanism linking hybrid learning and trainer competence to training effectiveness.
5. To propose strategies for optimizing the balance between digital and in-person training delivery.

## 3.2 Hypothesis of the Study

The study tests a serial multiple-mediation model in which Learner Engagement, Training Effectiveness, and Knowledge Retention each mediate the relationship between the constructs on either side of them, with Hybrid Learning and Trainer Competence positioned as parallel antecedents to Learner Engagement.

Each hypothesis below is stated as a null hypothesis (H0), tested for rejection, against its corresponding alternative hypothesis, following standard convention.

**Path hypotheses**

| # | Null hypothesis (H0) | Alternative hypothesis |
|---|---|---|
| H1 | Hybrid Learning has no significant effect on Learner Engagement. | Hybrid Learning has a significant positive effect on Learner Engagement. |
| H1b | Trainer Competence has no significant effect on Learner Engagement. | Trainer Competence has a significant positive effect on Learner Engagement. |
| H2 | Learner Engagement has no significant effect on Training Effectiveness. | Learner Engagement has a significant positive effect on Training Effectiveness. |
| H3 | Training Effectiveness has no significant effect on Knowledge Retention. | Training Effectiveness has a significant positive effect on Knowledge Retention. |
| H4 | Knowledge Retention has no significant effect on Job Performance. | Knowledge Retention has a significant positive effect on Job Performance. |

**Mediation hypotheses**

| # | Null hypothesis (H0) | Alternative hypothesis |
|---|---|---|
| H5a | Learner Engagement does not mediate the relationship between Hybrid Learning and Training Effectiveness. | Learner Engagement mediates the relationship between Hybrid Learning and Training Effectiveness. |
| H5b | Training Effectiveness does not mediate the relationship between Learner Engagement and Knowledge Retention. | Training Effectiveness mediates the relationship between Learner Engagement and Knowledge Retention. |
| H5c | Knowledge Retention does not mediate the relationship between Training Effectiveness and Job Performance. | Knowledge Retention mediates the relationship between Training Effectiveness and Job Performance. |

**Comparative hypotheses**

| # | Null hypothesis (H0) | Alternative hypothesis |
|---|---|---|
| H6 | There is no significant difference in Knowledge Retention between employees receiving blended training and employees receiving digital-only training. | Employees receiving blended training demonstrate significantly higher Knowledge Retention than employees receiving digital-only training. |
| H7 | There is no significant difference in Job Performance between employees receiving blended training and employees receiving digital-only or classroom-only training. | Employees receiving blended training demonstrate significantly higher Job Performance than employees receiving digital-only or classroom-only training. |
| H8 | The integration of digital and classroom training has no significant influence on Knowledge Retention and Job Performance. | The integration of digital and classroom training has a significant positive influence on Knowledge Retention and Job Performance. |

Trainer Competence is tested as a parallel antecedent alongside Hybrid Learning (H1b) rather than as a further step in the mediation chain, consistent with its treatment in Saroj, Sahney and Sekar (2026) — the closest match to this study's own mechanism, though this study's base paper for measurement purposes is Ye, Kuang and Liu (2022), discussed in Chapter 2, since Trainer Competence is not tested in that paper. Each hypothesis above is reported in Chapter 4 against its own null hypothesis specifically: a null hypothesis is rejected where the associated test statistic's p-value falls below 0.05, in which case the alternative hypothesis is accepted; where p exceeds 0.05, the null hypothesis is retained and the corresponding alternative hypothesis is not supported by this study's data.

## 3.3 Definition of Variables

The study's conceptual model comprises six constructs. **Hybrid Learning** and **Trainer Competence** serve as independent variables, each hypothesized to predict **Learner Engagement**, which functions as the first mediator in the model. Learner Engagement is hypothesized to predict **Training Effectiveness**, which in turn predicts **Knowledge Retention**, which finally predicts **Job Performance**, the study's ultimate dependent variable. Training Effectiveness and Knowledge Retention therefore each occupy a dual role, functioning simultaneously as an outcome of the constructs preceding them and a mediator of the relationship between those constructs and the outcomes that follow.

Hybrid Learning is operationalized across five dimensions — flexibility, accessibility, interaction, technology, and integration — reflecting the design-quality framework established by Boelens, De Wever and Voet (2017). Trainer Competence is operationalized across three dimensions, content knowledge, technical competence with digital delivery tools, and the trainer's ability to motivate participation, consistent with Saroj, Sahney and Sekar's (2026) treatment of instructor competence as a determinant of engagement; a fourth dimension commonly discussed in the trainer-competence literature, process competence, was not retained as a separate item once the instrument was compressed to the department's 30-item cap, discussed further in Section 3.9. Learner Engagement draws on the dedication and absorption components of engagement identified by Fredricks, Blumenfeld and Paris (2004). Training Effectiveness is operationalized following Kirkpatrick's (1994) framework, anchored specifically to the learning level: items assess the relevance of training content, the clarity with which it is understood, and the immediate applicability of what has been learned to the respondent's own job. This deliberately narrower operationalization distinguishes Training Effectiveness from Knowledge Retention and Job Performance, which correspond to Kirkpatrick's later behaviour and results levels and are measured as separate, later constructs in this study's chain rather than folded into a single effectiveness score. Knowledge Retention, measured as a multi-item self-report perception scale rather than an objective recall test, represents this study's most original contribution to measurement, as no validated multi-item retention scale of this kind was identified in the literature reviewed for this study. Job Performance is operationalized primarily at the task-performance level, following the distinction between task and contextual performance established by Williams and Anderson (1991) and operationalized in the Individual Work Performance Questionnaire (Koopmans et al., 2011, 2014).

## 3.4 Research Design

This study adopts a descriptive and explanatory quantitative research design, appropriate given its aim of both describing the current level of hybrid learning practice among the sampled employees and testing the hypothesized explanatory relationships between constructs. The explanatory component is necessary rather than optional: the study's central research question concerns not only the level of hybrid learning practice at Union Bank of India but the mechanism connecting it to training effectiveness, and testing a mediation model of this kind requires a design capable of estimating relationships between constructs, not simply reporting their levels.

The unit of analysis is the individual employee. Responses are analysed at this level throughout rather than aggregated to the branch or zone level, since the constructs measured, engagement, trainer competence, and self-reported performance among them, are properties of an individual employee's own experience of training rather than of the branch in which that employee works.

Data were collected at a single point in time, making this a cross-sectional rather than longitudinal design; this limits the study's ability to draw strong causal conclusions and is addressed further in the Limitations section of Chapter 5.

## 3.5 Sampling Methods

The population for this study consists of employees of Union Bank of India who have participated in training programmes combining digital and face-to-face delivery. Given the practical constraints of accessing a complete sampling frame within a public-sector banking organization, this study employs non-probability convenience sampling, consistent with the sampling approach adopted across comparable prior studies within this department.

Inclusion in the sample requires that a respondent has completed at least one training programme incorporating both a digital and a face-to-face component; employees who have experienced only a single delivery mode are excluded, since Hybrid Learning, and its Integration dimension specifically, cannot be meaningfully rated by a respondent without direct exposure to both modes.

The target sample size is approximately 80 respondents, the minimum specified by departmental research norms. This figure is also adequate for the study's actual analysis plan: Model-Alpha is tested as a chain of separate regression models rather than as a single combined structural model, and the largest of these models, predicting Learner Engagement from Hybrid Learning and Trainer Competence together, has only two predictors. Applying the commonly used rule of approximately ten cases per predictor for multiple regression, a sample of around twenty would be the practical minimum for this specific model, a threshold the target sample comfortably exceeds. The same rule, applied at a considerably less favourable ratio, was one basis for setting aside a more complex extended model during this study's design phase, discussed further as a future-research direction in Chapter 5.

## 3.7 Research Questions

1. How does blended learning affect knowledge retention compared with digital-only and classroom-only training?
2. Does blended learning improve employee performance after training?
3. Does learner engagement mediate the relationship between hybrid learning and training effectiveness, and does trainer competence also predict engagement?
4. How should organizations divide learning activities between digital and classroom formats?
5. What blended learning strategies can improve both retention and workplace application?

Research Questions 1 to 3 are answered through the hypothesis tests reported in Chapter 4: RQ1 corresponds to H6, RQ2 to H7, and RQ3 to H1b and H5a specifically. Research Questions 4 and 5 are practical rather than statistical in nature; they are not tested through a specific hypothesis but are addressed through the study's findings and recommendations in Chapter 5.

## 3.8 Data Analysis Techniques

Data were analyzed in the following sequence. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, and frequency distributions) were computed to characterize the sample and summarize construct-level responses. Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach's alpha for each of the six constructs. Bivariate relationships between constructs were examined using Pearson correlation. The path hypotheses (H1, H1b, H2, H3, H4) were tested using a series of four multiple regression models corresponding to each stage of the hypothesized chain.

The mediation hypotheses (H5a, H5b, H5c) were tested using Hayes' PROCESS macro (Model 6, serial multiple mediation) where this software is available and approved for use by the department; where it is not available, the Baron and Kenny (1986) causal-steps approach together with a Sobel test was used instead as the simpler alternative, a fallback already anticipated during instrument design. The comparative hypotheses (H6, H7) were tested using one-way ANOVA and independent-samples t-tests comparing respondents grouped by training delivery mode (digital-only, classroom-only, blended). H8 was tested using correlation and regression applied specifically to the Integration sub-dimension score against Knowledge Retention and Job Performance, rather than the full construct-level Hybrid Learning score used in H1.

## 3.9 Pilot Study, Reliability and Validity

A pilot study of approximately 20-30 respondents, drawn from Union Bank of India employees who are not included in the final sample, was conducted prior to full data collection, consistent with departmental precedent. The pilot served three purposes: assessing the reliability of the instrument prior to full-scale administration; identifying items that pilot respondents found unclear, ambiguous, or difficult to answer, so that wording could be revised before the main data collection; and confirming that the questionnaire could be completed within a reasonable time and, where administered digitally, that the platform itself presented no technical obstacles to completion.

Reliability was assessed using Cronbach's alpha, computed separately for each of the six constructs, with a threshold of .70 applied as the criterion for acceptable internal consistency, a more conservative threshold than the .60 sometimes accepted for exploratory research. Trainer Competence, reduced to three items during the instrument's compression to the department's 30-item cap, is the construct most likely to show a lower alpha given its reduced item count, and its reliability was examined first and with particular attention during the pilot analysis. If its alpha fell below the acceptable threshold, the planned remedy was item substitution from the original eight-item Trainer Competence set rather than the addition of new items, so as not to reopen the 30-item cap.

Content validity was established in two ways. First, the majority of the instrument's items are adapted from previously published, validated scales rather than developed from first principles: Hybrid Learning draws on the design-quality dimensions established by Boelens, De Wever and Voet (2017); Learner Engagement draws on Fredricks, Blumenfeld and Paris's (2004) dedication and absorption components; and Job Performance draws on the task-performance items validated in the Individual Work Performance Questionnaire (Koopmans et al., 2011, 2014). Knowledge Retention is the principal exception, developed specifically for this study in the absence of a suitable published multi-item scale, as discussed in Section 3.3. Second, the instrument as a whole was reviewed by the research supervisor prior to the pilot, checking each item against the construct it is intended to measure.

Face validity, the extent to which the instrument appears, on its surface, to measure what it claims to measure, was assessed during the pilot study by asking pilot respondents directly whether any item was unclear or difficult to interpret, in addition to the statistical reliability check described above.

Given the compressed nature of several constructs within the final 30-item instrument (see Appendix), dimension-level reliability could not be assessed for sub-dimensions represented by a single item; reliability was therefore assessed at the construct level only, a limitation acknowledged further in Chapter 5.
