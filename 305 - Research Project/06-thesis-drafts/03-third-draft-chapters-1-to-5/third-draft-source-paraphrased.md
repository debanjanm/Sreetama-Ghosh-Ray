# Hybrid Learning and Training Effectiveness at Union Bank of India

# Chapter 1 — Introduction

## 1.1 Background and Context

Bank staff are required to keep gaining knowledge and skills while carrying out their usual responsibilities. A modification in process, the introduction of a new digital platform, or the release of an updated product can influence the way they provide services and adhere to compliance standards. Training must be available during regular working hours and should provide value to employees when they come back to their job.

Digital platforms allow a bank to share educational resources with employees who work in various locations. Modules, videos, virtual sessions, assessments, and job aids are all available for use without the need to travel for each activity. That flexibility is important when a branch needs to keep serving customers. Access, however, is just the first step: finishing a module does not mean an employee has understood the procedure or knows how to apply it in their job. Some questions still require clarification from a trainer.

In-person sessions allow for asking questions, seeing demonstrations, practising, and receiving feedback. Employees are also able to talk about issues that occur within their respective branches. A classroom is not required for every update; a brief refresher or reference document might be more convenient to use online. What is important is the link between the two methods, allowing information shared digitally to be properly understood and applied during a trainer-led session or in the workplace.

Hybrid learning, also known as blended learning, intentionally combines both digital and in-person activities as part of a single programme. The digital section could bring up a subject before the session and remain available for later revision. A trainer can then use the session to explain unclear topics and practice job-related tasks. Without that connection, employees might see the activities as individual tasks instead of components of a single learning process.

This study focuses on Union Bank staff who are affiliated with the Regional Office Chennai South located in Royapettah, Chennai. The Bank’s annual reports describe Union Vidya, its Learning Management System (LMS), together with learning resources such as e-books, podcasts, audiobooks, microlearning content, virtual workshops, role-based learning programmes, mandatory e-learning for officers, and a Training Management System (Union Bank of India, 2024, 2025). These setups indicate that various methods of learning are provided. They do not provide information on how employees felt about their connection within a specific programme. Each participant therefore provided information about one recent programme that involved both digital and in-person learning.

Four constructs describe that experience. Hybrid learning connects the online and in-person activities, provides access to the platform and resources, and offers chances to review the material that was taught. Trainer Competence refers to the trainer’s understanding of the subject, their ability to explain things clearly, the use of appropriate tools, their encouragement of learners, and the feedback they provide. Learner Engagement reflects how much an employee is focused, involved, interested, actively taking part, and ready to seek further explanations. Training effectiveness is about how well the employee found the learning content clear, related to their work, helpful, and able to be used in their current role.

The conceptual framework is:

    Hybrid Learning ────────┐
    Trainer Competence ─────┼──→ Training Effectiveness
    Learner Engagement ────┘

Within the framework, Hybrid Learning, Trainer Competence, and Learner Engagement are three separate predictors of perceived Training Effectiveness. A well-structured programme and a skilled trainer do not guarantee the same level of involvement from each employee. Workload, available time, past experience, comfort with technology, level of motivation, and the specific branch requirements could also play a role. The questionnaire captures how employees experienced various aspects of a particular programme; the resulting connections are understood based on the limitations of a single self-reported survey.

The three fundamental papers each focus on a separate aspect of the question. Sosnova et al. (2025) investigate the effectiveness of hybrid education on student learning, offering a foundational conceptual framework for understanding this area. Kim (2022) focuses on automotive sales training and highlights the importance of delivery design, instructor participation, and hands-on practice within a corporate environment. Bahl, Kiran and Sharma (2024) evaluated training programmes for 402 employees at Indian banks by applying Kirkpatrick’s four-level framework, which includes Reaction, Learning, Behaviour, and Results. The current four-construct Union Bank model is not based on a single paper; instead, it draws from a mix of conceptual, workplace, and banking viewpoints.

For HR and learning and development personnel, the design of a programme is important because employees need to arrange their training around their work responsibilities. Disconnected activities may seem like an additional responsibility. A trainer who explains the importance of an activity and answers questions can help make participating in it simpler. The study looks at areas such as access, integration, trainer support, and engagement to highlight elements of training that the Bank can consider reviewing, while ensuring consistent service delivery.

## 1.2 Key Concepts and Definitions

### Hybrid Learning

Hybrid learning mixes online and in-person activities as part of a single organised programme. A bank could incorporate tools such as an LMS module, video content, virtual sessions, online evaluations, or downloadable reference materials as part of its digital training approach. Classroom discussion, demonstration, role play, group exercise, or supervised practice may form the in-person component. Each mode serves a specific role within the same learning process.

Employees assessed if the two modes felt connected, if they could access the platform and materials, and if the digital content stayed available for revision. The measure relates to their experience with one programme. It is not a technical evaluation of Union Vidya or a comparison of programmes offered through just one method.

### Trainer Competence

Trainer Competence refers to the extent to which a trainer is able to effectively support the learning process. Subject knowledge and clear explanations are important, along with the ability to link digital work with the live session, use appropriate tools, encourage participation, and offer helpful feedback. In a hybrid programme, employees might need the trainer to explain how an online task connects to future discussions or to a process in their own job.

An easy-to-use platform cannot take the place of a trainer’s explanation. A skilled trainer might still face limitations if the employees are unable to access the necessary learning materials. Evaluating these experiences individually allows for a comparison of how they are linked to the effectiveness of training.

### Learner Engagement

Learner Engagement refers to the qualities an employee contributes to the chosen programme, including their attention, effort, participation, interest, and readiness to ask questions. Attendance or simply having an online completion record is not sufficient to demonstrate actual participation. A participant might be there without paying close attention to the material; their involvement is shown through their effort to grasp the content and participate in the learning tasks.

The engagement questions focus on the behaviour of participants in a specific training programme. They do not evaluate general work engagement, organisational commitment, motivation, or metacognitive skills as separate factors.

### Training Effectiveness

Training effectiveness refers to how much the programme helped the employee in their current job. The questions focus on understanding the material, determining its relevance, evaluating confidence in the knowledge, and assessing the ability to use the learning in everyday tasks and related situations.

Training-evaluation and transfer literature supports this definition, which goes beyond simply asking whether employees liked a session. The evidence is based on self-reports: there is no measurement of any actual improvement in individual performance, customer service, productivity, reputation, or financial outcomes here.

### Learning Management System

A Learning Management System (LMS) assists an organisation in providing and overseeing learning by utilising digital tools like modules, readings, videos, quizzes, online meetings, and records of learning activities. Union Bank has identified Union Vidya as its Learning Management System, which is part of a larger system that includes e-learning, virtual training, staggered programmes, and role-based learning (Union Bank of India, 2024, 2025).

Union Vidya gives background information for the Hybrid Learning questions, and it is not treated as a separate score. The questionnaire refers to it as an example when asking about the platform used in the respondent’s chosen programme, as programmes and platform features can vary.

## 1.3 Need, Scope and Significance of the Study

### Need for the Study

The main issue is understanding how employees’ experiences with Hybrid Learning, the skill level of trainers, and the level of engagement from learners connect to their perception of how effective the training is. Bank employees gain knowledge as they handle customer interactions, follow established procedures, and carry out everyday tasks. If activities are hard to reach or not well connected, training may create more stress rather than assist in applying new knowledge.

Research on blended learning provides helpful insights for designing programmes, even though a lot of it focuses on students. A bank worker experiences a unique situation: customer service and compliance tasks continue as part of their training. Corporate studies provide evidence from the workplace, but there is still limited research on how these four constructs are experienced by employees working in an Indian public-sector bank.

Union Bank has recorded digital materials and offers training sessions led by trainers. Those records define the context, not the quality of an employee’s experience within a particular programme. The survey addresses that narrower question.

### Scope of the Study

Employees who were linked to Union Bank’s Regional Office Chennai South and had participated in a programme that included both digital and in-person learning within the last 12 months were considered eligible. Each person answered about one recent programme. A person who had only experienced one method was unable to understand the connection between the two and was therefore not qualified.

The four areas that were measured are Hybrid Learning, Trainer Competence, Learner Engagement, and Training Effectiveness. Other matters that may be relevant, such as knowledge retention, job performance, supervisor support, digital literacy, and the technical performance of Union Vidya, are not covered by this model. A narrower instrument helps keep the analysis focused and manageable for the selected sample.

Responses were gathered once using a structured questionnaire that included a five-point agreement format. Descriptive and correlational analyses provide an overview of this group of respondents and explain the connections between their ratings. The design cannot show whether training caused later changes in work behaviour or Bank performance.

### Significance of the Study

For Union Bank, the results organise employees’ feedback regarding the relationship between learning methods, trainer support, and level of participation. Lower-rated areas can help staff identify issues that should be reviewed, such as the timing of modules, access to learning materials, the level of guidance provided, the quality of feedback received, or opportunities to participate actively.

Trainers and those involved in learning and development can utilise these responses to move beyond just delivering content. Employees should be informed about the reason a digital module comes before a session, how a new procedure relates to their job, and where they can get help if they have questions later. Those practical links influence whether training seems feasible to handle along with everyday responsibilities.

The academic contribution is a specific analysis based on a public-sector banking environment. The questionnaire is based on conceptual, corporate, and Indian banking literature; it was reviewed by the guide, tested through piloting, and evaluated for internal consistency in the final sample.

## 1.4 Statement of the Problem

Union Bank’s learning system offers both digital materials and training sessions led by instructors, providing a mix of online and in-person learning opportunities. The presence of these resources does not necessarily reflect an individual employee’s experience. The main questions remain: do the activities seem related, do the trainers provide sufficient guidance, are the employees involved, and can they apply what they’ve learned in their current positions.

Evidence from higher education and corporate training provides context for these questions, but it does not directly look at the four constructs together within the selected Union Bank environment. In particular, published material about Union Vidya does not clarify how employees link its digital resources with trainer-led learning.

The study examines whether ratings of Hybrid Learning, Trainer Competence, and Learner Engagement jointly relate to perceived Training Effectiveness.

## 1.5 Research Questions

1. What are the participants’ views on Hybrid Learning, Trainer Competence, Learner Engagement, and Training Effectiveness?
2. What relationships are identified between the four study constructs?
3. To what degree do Hybrid Learning, Trainer Competence, and Learner Engagement jointly predict Training Effectiveness?

## 1.6 Industry and Company Context

### Banking Industry Context

Banking work involves handling regulations, digital technologies, financial products, and providing customer support. Employees are required to keep their understanding of procedures and risk controls up to date as they interact with customers on a daily basis. In a public-sector bank where staff are spread across different departments and locations, essential materials should be easily accessible while still allowing for opportunities to ask questions, practice, receive guidance, and later review the content.

Different training needs call for different activities. A brief policy update could be suitable for a digital module and a knowledge check; a new procedure might need a demonstration and hands-on practice; a customer-facing scenario may be more effectively discussed through role play. Digital material can remain available afterwards. The selection of the mode should be based on what the employees need to learn.

### Union Bank of India Context

Union Bank was established in Mumbai on 11th November 1919 and later became a nationalised bank in 1969. Andhra Bank and Corporation Bank joined the group through amalgamation on 1 April 2020 (Union Bank of India, n.d.-a). In June 2026, its public profile indicated that it had over 8,700 domestic branches and approximately 73,900 employees. In a workforce that operates in many different places, having access to up-to-date materials and support from trainers becomes a real challenge when it comes to learning.

### Union Bank Training Architecture

Union Bank backs learning by offering both physical training centres and digital learning options. The 2024–25 annual report outlines a Master Policy on Learning and Development that includes e-learning, virtual and staggered programmes, role-based training, mandatory e-learning for officers, and a Training Management System under Union Vidya (Union Bank of India, 2025). The previous annual report highlights Union Vidya as the Bank’s Learning Management System and documents digital resources and virtual workshops along with classroom and on-site programmes (Union Bank of India, 2024).

Nine specialised Union Learning Academies were introduced in 2022. The Bank’s public list also includes a Staff Training College located in Bengaluru and seven Staff Training Centres in other locations (Union Bank of India, 2022; Union Bank of India, n.d.-b). Along with the digital resources, these are included in its learning setup. Their presence does not show who went to the centre or if each programme used both methods.

A circular issued on 9 September 2026 serves as an example of how different modes are connected. Classroom sessions held at Union Learning Academies and Zonal Learning Centres can be viewed via Microsoft Teams. Schedules and recordings are made available via Union Vidya, and virtual participants can submit questions through the chat, which the programme coordinator will address at appropriate times (Union Bank of India, 2026). This setup allows access to certain trainer-led sessions; the circular does not include any information on how employees performed throughout the Bank.

The survey therefore remains focused on the experiences that employees can share: one recent hybrid programme participated in by eligible respondents was connected with the Regional Office Chennai South. The findings are directed towards that specific group, not the entire Bank.

# Chapter 2 — Review of Literature

## 2.1 Review of Relevant Literature

### Hybrid Learning as an Integrated Training Design

In workplace training, hybrid learning involves more than simply allocating time for an online module and a classroom session. The activities should be organised in a way that employees can follow along and relate them to their own tasks. A digital task should have a clear purpose for being placed before or after a trainer-led activity.

Digital delivery allows the distribution of fundamental information, brief evaluations, and reference materials to different locations. In-person time can be utilised for asking questions, demonstrating concepts, engaging in practice, and receiving feedback. Employees tend to view a programme as a single learning experience when they clearly understand how the different activities are related.

Garrison and Kanuka (2004) emphasise that integration is a key focus in blended learning. Graham (2006) talks about how different ways of delivering instruction, teaching strategies, and learning techniques can be combined. Although both sources are from education, they provide a useful design test for employee training: the activities should complement each other, not repeat or contradict one another.

Two educational reviews assist in defining the larger question about learning. Vo, Zhu and Diep (2017) combined data from 51 effect sizes related to higher-education courses and found that blended learning had a small but consistent advantage over traditional classroom instruction, with an average effect size of g = 0.385. Yu, Yu, Li and Wang (2025) conducted a review of 133 studies involving 18,464 students and discovered that the intervention had a moderate to large positive impact on learning outcomes, with an effect size of g = 0.651. These are student results. Bank workers frequently need to keep up with customer service tasks, tight deadlines, and new procedures, which means the estimates serve as general information rather than specific predictions for Union Bank.

Kumar and Pande (2017) look at how learning happens through technology for working professionals. They assess the institutional, facilitator, learner, and pedagogical factors that exist within a blended-learning environment. Their account is helpful here because simply having access to a platform does not explain if employees find the content relevant, if they receive guidance, or if they apply what they learn to their work.

Mulaudzi’s (2021) Master’s dissertation explores the use of blended teaching methods in training programmes within the transport sector of South Africa. The conversation about time, access, flexibility, materials, and practical obstacles is important for employees who learn outside of regular work hours. The themes influenced the way the current questionnaire was written regarding access and connected learning, although the dissertation tool was not used as a pre-existing corporate measurement scale.

The Perceptions of the Blended Learning Environment Questionnaire, developed by Han and Ellis in 2020, explores how learners perceive the relationship between different modes of learning and the role that online learning plays in this context. The language used in higher education is not appropriate for direct communication with bank employees. The main ideas—such as connection, access, availability of materials, and the ability to revise—help clarify the content of the Hybrid Learning items.

Sosnova, Hlianenko, Sosnova, Tsyna and Tsyna (2025) present the foundational conceptual paper. Their experiment involved 64 students pursuing higher education, who participated in activities that combined online platforms with practical, problem-based, interactive, and project-oriented learning approaches. The results showed that the hybrid group performed better than the comparison group in terms of learning outcomes. The population and outcome measures used in the paper are different from those in this thesis, and the paper does not include a Union Bank questionnaire. It is applied to the more specific idea that hybrid design and learning effectiveness can be examined together.

### Hybrid Training and Effectiveness in Corporate Settings

Kim (2022) supplies the corporate training evidence. In South Korean automotive sales training, the way content is delivered has shifted to include traditional methods, fully digital formats, and hybrid approaches. The analysis includes 583 training instances from 2019 to 2020, consisting of 325 traditional sessions, 140 fully digital sessions, and 118 hybrid sessions. One key difference between the hybrid format and the more self-directed fully digital format is that the hybrid format includes both online and offline activities.

The training was assessed using the CIP-R framework developed by Kim, which encompasses content, instructional design, programmed learning, and recommendation. This framework focuses on aspects such as the content itself, the design of the instructional materials, personalised learning experiences, learners’ perceived competence, and the effectiveness of recommendations provided. Reported effectiveness decreased when training transitioned from traditional methods to a fully digital format, but it increased again after the introduction of hybrid delivery. One of the factors linked to the reported outcome was how much the instructor interacted, the length of the session, the personalised learning experience, the level of confidence and ability gained, and the extent to which the skills were relevant to the job.

The sequence in which Kim’s group organised their training matters: traditional training was considered the most effective, followed by hybrid training, and fully digital training was ranked as the least effective. The results showed that there was no significant statistical difference between the hybrid and fully digital delivery methods. Kim, therefore, cannot back up a broad statement that hybrid training is better than other methods. This paper is more helpful in demonstrating the importance of considering delivery design, instructor engagement, and practical application when assessing a corporate programme.

Kim’s findings inform the present measurement choices. Training effectiveness goes beyond just a good feeling right away; hybrid learning focuses on how well the different methods work together; and trainer expertise stays important because having digital materials doesn’t take the place of personal guidance and feedback.

The term "hybrid" does not indicate whether an employee is able to access the materials, prepare adequately in advance, take part in the session, or have the opportunity to practice. These details are particularly important when staff members come from various branches while the usual work is still ongoing. For that reason, the Union Bank questionnaire includes questions about a programme’s related activities and the support that is available. It does not ask employees to compare formats they might never have used.

Kim compares corporate delivery arrangements. This thesis instead asks individual bank employees if a particular programme helped them understand and apply work-related learning. The designs should not be expected to yield exactly the same outcomes, but the comparison at the corporate level makes it worthwhile to consider the question at the employee level.

Uddin, Ahamed, Jakowan, Islam and Nahar (2026) conducted a study involving 150 employees who had experience with blended learning. They looked into how the development of soft skills and the gain of knowledge relate to how well employees perform. Both factors related to learning showed positive connections with blended learning, but the direct effect of these factors on performance was not significant once the other connections were taken into account. Their result suggests that you should not assume the outcome of work is determined just by how it is delivered.

Uddin et al. provide supporting workplace literature. Testing their specific soft skills, knowledge gain, and performance paths would need methods that are not included in this four-construct survey.

### Trainer Competence in Hybrid Training

When transitioning from a platform-based training to an in-person session, the trainer assists employees in understanding the order of the training activities. One thing that can make a difference is having clear explanations of the subject, connecting the activities, using the right digital tools, asking questions, and getting feedback. Without this support, finishing an online task might not be properly linked to the employee’s job duties.

The quality of the technical platform and the skill of the trainer are separate issues. Some materials might be found online without any information on why they’re there; even an experienced trainer could find it difficult if employees cannot access those materials. The questionnaire therefore includes separate sections on programme design and trainer support.

One thing that is not included in this specific workplace model is the combination of Trainer Competence and Learner Engagement through base paper tests. Kim (2022) talks about the roles of instructors, the importance of participation, the value of practice, and the aspects of design in corporate training. Mulaudzi (2021) discusses the challenges related to facilitating and overcoming practical access barriers in the workplace. In a university context, Ansari and colleagues (2023) link blended-learning training to the competence and satisfaction of both lecturers and students. That population is different from bank employees, but the study provides support for looking at facilitation rather than considering technology availability as sufficient.

These sources assist in separating the trainer’s input from the employee’s reaction. The Trainer Competence areas include explaining concepts, using digital tools effectively, linking different modes of learning, helping participants engage, and giving useful feedback. The Learner Engagement elements include attention, effort, participation, asking questions, and showing interest. A good trainer rating does not necessarily mean that the learners are actively participating.

### Learner Engagement

Engagement refers to being actively involved, which involves giving attention, putting in effort, taking part, showing interest, and seeking clarification when needed. A login or attendance record does not indicate if someone attempted to grasp a new process. Completion therefore offers an incomplete picture of the learning process.

Schaufeli, Bakker and Salanova (2006) define work engagement as consisting of three key elements: vigour, dedication, and absorption. Their size does not indicate how much someone is involved in a single training programme. The current items focus solely on the general concept of active effort: respondents share their level of attention, participation, involvement, questioning, and interest during the chosen programme.

Learner Engagement remains an independent factor because the design of the programme and the skills of the trainer do not completely control how much attention or involvement an employee shows. Time pressure, past experience, confidence with digital tools, personal motivation, and the specific conditions of the branch can all influence the response. The model therefore looks at how engagement is connected to training effectiveness instead of assuming it follows a set order among the other factors and the result.

### Training Effectiveness

Kirkpatrick’s framework divides the evaluation process into four stages: reaction, learning, behaviour, and results. That difference stops a good response to a session from representing all the results of training. An employee might experience a session without fully grasping its content, or grasp the content without later having the opportunity to use what they’ve learned.

Bahl, Kiran and Sharma (2024) present a paper that serves as the foundation for the Indian banking sector. Their evaluation, which is open to all, includes 402 employees from both managerial and non-managerial roles across public, private, and foreign banks, and it is based on Kirkpatrick’s four levels. It highlights the importance of assessing training programmes within the banking sector. The paper does not look at hybrid delivery methods, trainer skills, learner involvement, or Union Vidya, which is why it cannot provide the current model on its own.

Holton (1996) suggests that the different levels of evaluation alone cannot fully explain how findings are applied in real-world settings. Baldwin and Ford (1988) explain that transfer occurs when learning is generalised and maintained in the workplace. Blume and their team in 2010 discovered connections between the process of transferring knowledge and various factors, including the learner’s personal traits, how the training was structured, and the conditions of the workplace. These sources warn that the effectiveness of later applications is not solely based on the training event itself.

The result being measured here is more specific: employees assess how well they understand the content, find it relevant, feel capable of using it, see its use in their work, get help with related issues, and feel confident in their current roles. They are not required to estimate productivity, customer satisfaction, reputation, or financial results for the Bank, as this would need additional data.

Aziz’s (2015) General Training Effectiveness Scale was created specifically for use in workplace learning within Malaysia. The difference between individual learning and organisational results guides the current questionnaire’s focus on employees. The wording from Union Bank was checked for content, tested in a trial, and evaluated using reliability analysis.

Bahl, Kiran and Sharma (2024) examine a wider chain that encompasses the outcomes. This survey does not have its own records regarding the productivity of branches, the results for customers, or the patterns of behaviour seen over time. It could also ask if employees understood the material, realised its importance, and felt confident in applying it. That is an outcome observed at the employee level, rather than a complete Kirkpatrick evaluation.

Aziz (2015) also ensures that individual learning remains separate from the achievements of an organisation. A person cannot fairly assess the overall outcome of the bank based on just one recent programme. The job performance was not included in the model for a related reason: actual performance requires a suitable measure and evidence that goes beyond the same employee’s account of training.

### Workplace Implementation Conditions

An LMS can store content, but individuals still have to determine which materials should be reviewed prior to a session, what topics require discussion, and when feedback is necessary. A classroom discussion might not provide employees with materials that they can review again later. This link between activities is key to the Hybrid Learning measure.

Mubayrik’s 2018 review of workplace blended learning highlights several common themes, including flexible access, real-world activities, teamwork, learner satisfaction, and technological support. The studies differ in terms of the population they involve, the way they are structured, and how they measure outcomes. The diversity of options is a key factor in considering the task, trainer, technology, and the chance to take part in a specific environment, rather than assuming that a single method of delivery is equally effective in all situations.

The order of tasks is important when employees need to allocate time for training while handling their regular work responsibilities. A digital activity may present a policy or example prior to a live session that focuses on cases and practical applications. A trainer could start by teaching a challenging subject and then provide digital resources for review later. The questionnaire asks if the selected programme’s activities seemed connected and easy to reach; it does not list or order these possible sequences.

One option is to prepare digitally, practice with a trainer, apply skills in the workplace, and review using digital tools. This example shows how a connected programme could appear. It is not another model that was tested in the survey.

### Use of the Literature in Practical Recommendations

Recommendations should start by presenting the actual ratings given by respondents, followed by an analysis of those ratings using existing literature. Low access or material scores suggest issues with the timing and availability of resources. Lower scores in terms of explanation or feedback may indicate that the trainer needs to review their preparation. Limited involvement or asking for more information would focus on questions, practice, and follow-up.

Employee ratings highlight where a programme is strong or where it might be challenging; the reviewed studies explain why these areas are important.

### Summary of the Empirical Review

The sources perform complementary roles. Sosnova et al. (2025) link hybrid methods with learning effectiveness in education; Kim (2022) examines corporate training design, instructors, practice, and outcomes; and Bahl, Kiran and Sharma (2024) assess training among Indian bank employees. Han and Ellis (2020), Mulaudzi (2021), and Aziz (2015) provide the themes and language used in the questionnaire related to the workplace.

No single source has the full model or a ready-made instrument for Union Bank. Appendix B explains how the scored items are based on these different areas of literature.

### Comparison of the Three Base Papers

The foundational papers examine three different scenarios instead of focusing on a single, identical model. Sosnova et al. (2025) conducted their research with students; Kim (2022) focused on automotive sales trainees; and Bahl, Kiran and Sharma (2024) studied Indian bank employees, but none of these studies included a hybrid-delivery approach. Their differences cause the Union Bank’s question to be significant, but neither provides a result that can be directly copied in Chennai.

Their methods also set limits on comparison. Sosnova and Kim examine different arrangements or results under various delivery conditions, whereas Bahl and their team apply a multi-level approach to evaluate outcomes. The existing single-use survey includes four ratings and evaluates one regression. It cannot demonstrate that altering Union Bank’s delivery method would recreate a previous outcome or achieve results at the organisational level.

Kim’s conversation about instructors and participation, Mulaudzi’s (2021) account of facilitation and time in the workplace, and Schaufeli, Bakker and Salanova’s (2006) idea of active effort all contribute to distinguishing between the support provided by trainers and the actions taken by learners. The final source relates to overall work engagement, which is used as background information instead of a specific tool for training. A bank worker might find a trainer’s explanation helpful but may not have enough time to get ready or ask questions. The two experiences are therefore measured separately.

The sources used for measurement serve different purposes. Han and Ellis (2020) raise questions regarding the relationship between modes, Mulaudzi (2021) discusses the conditions related to workplace access and Aziz (2015) focuses on outcomes at the employee level. Appendix B documents how those themes were applied to a specific Union Bank programme. Neither a student-scale tool nor a transport-sector instrument can become a validated Bank measure just by altering its wording.

The literature points to a single focused test: among employees who experienced both methods, do more positive evaluations of integration, trainer skill, and involvement relate to a higher sense of training effectiveness? The response can highlight areas that need to be checked, but the single design does not address the order of events or cause and effect.

## 2.2 Theoretical Framework

### Blended-Learning Integration

The framework starts by incorporating the learning modes outlined by Garrison and Kanuka (2004) and Graham (2006). Digital work and a live session should be arranged in a clear and logical order. When employees understand the connection, it becomes clearer why each activity is done.

For an employee, the process might begin with digital content that introduces a topic, followed by a session focused on real cases and practical application. The material might still be accessible if it needs to be reviewed again later. A programme might also start with a live explanation and then use digital tools for further support. What is important is whether employees understand how their activities relate to the needs of the work.

The design of a programme cannot be simplified to the platform it is built on. Posting a module does not guarantee that it will adequately prepare staff for discussions; even a well-designed classroom activity might be difficult to apply effectively in the future without a reliable reference resource. The Hybrid Learning components also inquire about both connectivity and access, not just a login or completion status.

### Separate Employee-Level Predictors

The second viewpoint maintains three connected experiences as separate. Kim (2022) talks about the aspects of design, the role of instructors, how participants engage, and the importance of practice in corporate training. Ansari and others in 2023, along with Mulaudzi in 2021, provide additional evidence regarding facilitation and access in different learning modes. Employee engagement can also be influenced by situations that are outside the control of either the trainer or the programme. that is why each of the three is measured individually and then all three are inputted together into the regression analysis.

An employee may receive the material and understand the trainer’s instructions, yet is unable to take part due to the demands of their branch’s work responsibilities. Another person might be enthusiastic about learning but struggle to locate the digital resource. Combining these situations into a single positive or negative rating would obscure which specific part of the experience requires improvement.

The predictors can still move together. Clear guidance can assist employees in completing a digital task, and engaging activities can encourage them to take part. The model enables these connections without requiring that one predictor must lead to engagement in a specific order. Its regression explores how the three ratings are connected to Training Effectiveness when looked at as a group.

### Training Evaluation and Transfer

Training evaluation and transfer offer the third viewpoint. Kirkpatrick (1994) differentiates between the immediate response and subsequent results. Holton (1996), Baldwin and Ford (1988), and Blume et al. (2010) talk about how the learner, the design, and the work environment are connected to the application. The result here is the employee’s ability to clearly understand, relate to, find value in, and apply the learning, rather than a result that applies to the whole organisation.

“Effectiveness” can otherwise mean several different things. Enjoying a trainer’s session is a reaction; grasping a concept is learning; applying it to a task is application. A change that affects the entire bank in terms of service or productivity is even more comprehensive. The questionnaire focuses on understanding, the relevance of roles, and the reported use or capability of applying the learning.

Transfer literature also highlights the events that take place following a session. An employee may know how to carry out a procedure but might not have the chance, the time, or the assistance needed to apply it right away. A single survey cannot track a skill’s development over several months; it can only note if respondents perceive a link between their training and their current job responsibilities (Baldwin and Ford, 1988; Holton, 1996).

### Conceptual Framework

The theoretical perspectives support the following framework:

    Hybrid Learning ────────┐
    Trainer Competence ─────┼──→ Training Effectiveness
    Learner Engagement ────┘

Hybrid Learning, Trainer Competence, and Learner Engagement are all included in the model as separate and distinct variables. The arrows show the SPSS analysis of predictors and outcomes, but they do not indicate the order of the predictors or show that one causes the other.

Each section of the questionnaire serves a specific function: organising and accessing information, facilitating the trainer’s role, encouraging employee involvement, or assessing the perceived learning and relevance. Separate blocks allow you to identify a weaker part of the experience even when the overall ratings are high. The regression provides the joint test; item responses highlight important issues that need to be reviewed.

The participants were employees associated with the Union Bank’s Regional Office in Chennai South. The policy outlines ten mandatory e-learning modules that all officers must complete. These resources make Union Bank a suitable place to examine hybrid learning, but having good infrastructure alone does not explain how participants experienced the programme. The survey therefore seeks input from eligible employees regarding a recent programme that utilised both methods, including the access and support they encountered.

Respondents evaluate their own programme instead of the whole training system. They are not required to compare different programmes or share customer details, private records, or overall bank performance information.

## 2.4 Gaps in the Study

The gap between the workplace and academic settings occurs because a lot of the evidence and research on blended learning comes from higher education. Employees train in line with their schedules, customer requirements, and the stress of their work environment. Corporate studies do exist, but they offer less direct evidence regarding these experiences compared to the student literature.

The gap in the banking sector still exists, even after the study conducted by Bahl, Kiran, and Sharma in 2024 on the effectiveness of training for Indian bank employees. Their model does not cover Hybrid Learning, Trainer Competence, Learner Engagement, or Union Vidya. Union Bank’s public documents mention the learning resources they offer but do not explain how employees felt about using different methods within a single programme.

The gap between prediction and combination refers to what the three foundational papers do not address collectively. Sosnova examines hybrid approaches in education, Kim analyzes different ways corporations organise their delivery systems, and Bahl, Kiran, and Sharma assess training programmes within the banking sector. No study examines the three separate constructs collectively in relation to training effectiveness at the employee level within a public-sector bank.

The gap in measurements arises due to the different scales available: one tool was created for assessing students’ experiences in blended learning, while another scale focuses on workplace effectiveness and includes outcomes at the organisational level. Neither fits this short bank survey unchanged. The four sections of the questionnaire were based on existing literature, checked against the guide, and tested online with 20 qualified employees between September 15th and 16th, 2026. SPSS provides an overall measure of consistency for the 26 items, but it does not offer a separate reliability assessment for each individual construct.

The contribution is at the point where these different areas meet: the employee experience with hybrid delivery, trainer support, participation, and how effective they feel the learning process is, specifically within a public-sector bank that uses Union Vidya as part of its learning system.

# Chapter 3 — Research Methodology

## 3.1 Objectives of the Study

This study explores how eligible Union Bank employees experienced a particular hybrid training programme and examines the relationship between their evaluations and their perception of the training’s effectiveness. They might use digital resources and participate in sessions led by trainers while still carrying out their regular banking duties. The measure is based on their understanding of that programme, not just having an LMS.

The primary objective is:

To assess if Hybrid Learning, Trainer Competence, and Learner Engagement jointly predict Training Effectiveness among eligible respondents of Union Bank of India.

The specific objectives are:

1. To describe how respondents perceive Hybrid Learning, Trainer Competence, Learner Engagement, and Training Effectiveness.
2. To look into the connections between the four study concepts.
3. To figure out how Hybrid Learning, Trainer Competence, and Learner Engagement jointly predict Training Effectiveness.

These objectives focus on the relationships and statistical predictions observed in the chosen sample, without implying that one event causes another.

## 3.3 Definition of Variables

Four constructs are scored from the questionnaire.

Hybrid learning involves the integration of both digital and in-person activities within a single programme. Questions 5 through 11 focus on the design, access to the platform, available materials, and subsequent revisions.

Trainer Competence includes having good subject knowledge, explaining things clearly, connecting different teaching methods, using appropriate digital tools, encouraging learners, and providing helpful feedback. It is measured by Questions 12–17.

Learner Engagement describes how much attention, effort, involvement, questions, and interest a participant shows while taking part in a specific programme. Questions 18–23 measure it. Engagement stays as an independent factor because factors like workload, available time, experience, motivation, confidence in using digital tools, and local circumstances can influence participation in ways that go beyond the design of the programme or the support provided by the trainer.

Training effectiveness records measure how well learners understand the content, how relevant the training is to their job roles, their ability to apply what they’ve learned, their confidence in using the knowledge, and their ability to use it in related tasks. Questions 24–30 ask about these employee-level outcomes.

## 3.4 Research Design

The design involves quantitative, descriptive, correlational, and predictive elements in terms of statistical analysis. Structured ratings explain how responses are organised; correlations indicate the direction and strength of relationships between pairs of variables; and SPSS regression evaluates how three independent variables collectively relate to Training Effectiveness.

Each participant filled out the survey once regarding a hybrid programme they had attended within the last 12 months. Mentioning a single programme reduces the necessity of combining memories from multiple different courses. The individual response record serves as the unit of analysis.

    Hybrid Learning ────────┐

The predictors might be connected, but the model does not suggest that the way a programme is designed or the skill of the trainer is the main factor in determining engagement. Employee and workplace conditions that are not covered in the questionnaire may also play a role.

The population of interest consisted of employees from Union Bank’s Regional Office in Chennai South who had participated in a programme that combined both digital and in-person learning methods within the last 12 months.

Purposive, non-probability sampling was used to select employees who had the necessary experience to answer the questions. Only individuals who had participated in both methods during the specified time frame were considered eligible, as the survey explores the relationship between the two.

The collection was restricted to offices and branches that were available for visitation during the internship period. The eligibility question then checked for relevant training experience. Respondents were not selected randomly from a complete list of all Bank employees, meaning the percentages and connections shown cannot be generalised statistically to every Union Bank employee.

Screening left 103 profile-complete records. SPSS utilised 102 valid cases for the multiple regression analysis because one record was missing the Trainer Competence mean in the analysis file.

No employee name or number was requested. Each qualified form was considered as a single response entry; identical answer patterns alone were not enough to confirm that the same person submitted the form more than once.

The total number of employees who were approached or received the link was not fully documented, making it impossible to provide an official response rate. Table 3.3 instead considers the forms that were received and kept.

## 3.7 Research Instrument and Scoring

Appendix A contains the self-administered 30-question instrument. Questions 1 through 4 determine if someone is eligible and gather information about their profile. Questions 5 to 30 ask the respondent to rate their agreement on a scale from Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree. Respondents mentioned one hybrid programme from the past 12 months.

The themes of the items are derived from the foundational papers and relevant measurement literature, with the adaptation process detailed in Appendix B. Union Vidya has been identified as the bank’s LMS and is provided as an example of a platform that a respondent might have used.

The four question blocks cover different sections of the same programme. Questions 5 through 11 focus on how materials are delivered and accessed; questions 12 through 17 deal with the support provided by the trainer; questions 18 through 23 relate to the employee’s personal participation; and questions 24 through 30 cover the learning outcomes and their usefulness in the workplace. This split gives more insight than a single general opinion about the programme.

Questions 5 through 30 are rated on a scale from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). In SPSS, the four construct means are referred to as HLTMEAN, TCTMEAN, LETMEAN, and TETMEAN.

Each construct score is calculated as the average of the items it is assigned, and it stays on the 1 to 5 scale; higher scores reflect more positive evaluations. All the items move in the same direction, so none of them need to be reversed. Means enable the comparison of blocks that contain six or seven items. They summarise the respondents’ answers, not the observed job performance or the Bank’s overall results.

## 3.11 Ethical Considerations and Limitations

Participation was voluntary. The questionnaire did not ask for any personal information or sensitive bank details, and participants were informed that only the combined results would be used for academic research.

Several design limits affect interpretation. The non-probability sample is not suitable for statistically representing all bank employees, and a single self-reported survey cannot establish a causal relationship. Having the same person evaluate both the programme experience and the outcome can help make the observed connections stronger. The saved SPSS output includes an overall Cronbach’s alpha for 26 items, but does not provide separate alphas for individual constructs. These small differences in the items were not subjected to significance testing, but they indicate the importance of examining how trainers discuss digital preparation and how feedback assists employees in addressing misconceptions.

#### Learner Engagement responses

Engagement levels were favourable in terms of attention, effort, participation, involvement, asking questions, and showing interest. On each of the questions Q20, Q21, and Q23, 93 out of 102 respondents chose either agree or strongly agree. This aligns with Learner Engagement having the highest construct mean, as it is most often described as active involvement in the programme they selected.

Question-seeking showed a slightly different pattern. On question 22, 88 respondents agreed or strongly agreed that they sought clarification, while eleven responded with a neutral stance. One thing that could be neutral is when there was no need for further explanation, no opportunity to start a conversation, or when the employee paused or was unsure. One thing the questionnaire cannot do is tell apart those options, so the real question is whether the programmes make it simple to ask.

Positive trainer and engagement ratings come from the same sample, but they reflect different experiences. One issue relates to the support provided; the other relates to what employees claim they did. The analysis checks if the latter still has a unique link to Training Effectiveness, along with trainer competence and hybrid design.

#### Training Effectiveness responses

Training effectiveness includes items that cover understanding, relevance, capability, application, task support, and confidence. In Figure 4.8, 93 people said they either agreed or strongly agreed that they understood the main knowledge or skills (Q24). The total is 89 for each of the questions Q25 through Q28. These are the employees’ own accounts of what they have learned and how they might apply it, rather than observations made by their supervisors regarding their performance.

On Q29, 88 respondents indicated that training helped them deal with relevant tasks or problems more effectively—five less than the number who found it helpful for understanding content in Q24. This difference was not significance-tested. It still explains why understanding and the application of work are questioned separately. Q30 received 87 favourable and ten neutral responses regarding confidence in responsibilities; confidence is still seen as a perception rather than a measurable improvement in job performance.

Across Figures 4.5 to 4.8, there is a general agreement, although it is not entirely consistent across all cases. One thing that is important is that neutral and negative responses are significant because a high average score might hide different experiences for some employees. The item counts show what respondents chose, not the reasons behind their choices; any action based on their selections requires additional evaluation.

## 4.3 Interpretation of Results

Employees who felt more involved in the chosen programme also provided higher scores for how relevant it was, how confident they felt, and how useful it was for their work. For bank employees, participation might involve closely following a session, asking questions, practising a procedure, or reviewing the material again when a task comes up.

Hybrid Learning received a positive rating and was found to be related to the effectiveness of training. Yet, question six highlights a relatively weaker aspect: digital activities did not adequately prepare respondents for the subsequent face-to-face session, as compared to other Hybrid Learning components that received higher ratings. Time or guidance may be required, but the survey is unable to determine the cause. It suggests that preparation is something that should be examined or considered for review.

The analysis indicates that each predictor has a positive relationship with Training Effectiveness. Regression examines if a relationship stays significant even after considering other variables in the model. By that more specific standard, Learner Engagement is clearly the most prominent one.

## 4.4 Comparison with Previous Studies

Sosnova and her team in 2025 examined the use of hybrid methods and how they affected students’ learning results. The Union Bank respondents also had a positive view of connected learning, but there are differences in the populations studied, the measures used, and the design of the studies. No classroom-only comparison group was surveyed here.

Kim’s 2022 automotive study focuses on delivery methods, instructors, and course design, and it shows that hybrid delivery did not consistently perform better than traditional training in all comparisons. In this context, both Hybrid Learning and Trainer Competence are positively related to Training Effectiveness, but Learner Engagement shows the strongest correlation in the combined model. This bank survey focuses on how employees view a particular programme, not on Kim’s comparison of different ways the programme is delivered.

Bahl, Kiran and Sharma (2024) assess bank training using Kirkpatrick’s levels. The existing evidence is limited: respondents indicate they understand, find relevant, and perceive the learning as useful, but there is no data on actual performance or outcomes at the organisational level. Mulaudzi’s (2021) discussion on access to the workplace and practical obstacles provides a reason to examine more closely the lower digital preparedness score in Table 4.3.

The number of responses shows the difference between learning and applying knowledge: 93 people gave a positive rating for their understanding on question 24, while 88 reported feeling more confident in handling tasks or solving problems on question 29. The difference is minor and not yet tested, but the questions focus on different results. Kirkpatrick and transfer research show that having understanding alone does not guarantee that someone will continue to act in a work-related way; this survey captures a single instance of an employee’s perspective.

Han and Ellis (2020) examine how students engage with a blended learning environment. Q6 provides a workplace example of why this is important: even though Hybrid Learning was generally viewed positively, the preparation done before the in-person session received the lowest average rating in that category. The question is related to bank training, but it does not consider the student instrument as approved for use by bank employees.

## 4.5 Theoretical and Practical Implications

One important factor in how effective training is seen to be is the combination of good programme design, support from trainers, and active participation from employees. Their correlations are strong enough that individual pairings should not be considered as distinct adjusted effects. The cross-sectional results do not indicate any cause-and-effect relationships between them.

The most straightforward practical observation is the transition from digital setup to the actual live session. Coordinators can verify when materials arrive at employees, assess if work schedules provide enough time to use them, and evaluate how trainers incorporate the material into their sessions. Questions and practice opportunities should be available throughout the session. Q6 and the engagement association encourage these proposals, but the survey did not evaluate the suggested changes.

The process of getting ready, the session itself, and the follow-up review can all be considered as part of a single ongoing sequence. Before starting a session, employees should have a clear digital task and sufficient time to complete it. During the session, the trainer can link the task to a banking example and encourage participants to ask questions. Afterward, available resources allow staff to review the subject whenever their work demands it. The questionnaire includes these stages; a future assessment would need to determine if modifying them leads to better results.

# Chapter 5 — Summary, Conclusion and Suggestions

## 5.1 Introduction

This chapter discusses the significance of the findings in relation to the chosen respondents from Union Bank. Hybrid Learning, Trainer Competence, and Learner Engagement are the independent variables, and Training Effectiveness is the dependent variable. The regression uses 102 valid cases.

## 5.2 Summary of the Study

Union Bank offers training for its employees using both physical locations and digital resources, such as Union Vidya and virtual training methods. A programme might therefore include online content, guided discussions by a trainer, hands-on practice, opportunities to ask questions, and materials that can be used later.

The study focused on four constructs:

- Hybrid Learning;
- Trainer Competence;
- Learner Engagement; and
- Training Effectiveness.

Respondents looked at one hybrid programme from the past 12 months. The instrument included four questions about eligibility and profile along with 26 items that asked for agreement on a five-point scale. A separate online pilot was conducted from 15 to 16 September 2026, while the main collection started on 15 September. It assessed clarity, relevance, repetition, time burden, and the use of Union Vidya wording; responses from the pilot phase were not included in the main analysis.

Paper forms and Google Forms resulted in 132 primary submissions from employees associated with the Regional Office Chennai South and selected branches. Screening left 103 profile-complete records. SPSS reported 102 valid cases for construct-level analysis and regression work because there was one missing value for the Trainer Competence mean in the file.

The hypothesis is asking if the three separate factors together can predict how effective the training is. The saved SPSS output contains descriptives, one complete reliability analysis, exploratory factor analysis results, correlation matrices, and multiple regression outputs. Regression supplies the hypothesis decision.

## 5.3 Major Findings

### 5.3.1 Respondents rated all four areas positively

Every construct mean exceeded the scale midpoint. Learner Engagement had the highest score at 4.317, followed by Training Effectiveness with 4.254, Trainer Competence at 4.158, and Hybrid Learning with 4.148. Most respondents provided positive feedback about the chosen programme.

Hybrid Learning remained positive but ranked lowest. Merging an online platform with an in-person session might result in employees needing more guidance or a stronger link between the content and the training activities led by the instructor.

### 5.3.2 The four constructs were positively associated

Each pairwise Pearson correlation was found to be positive and statistically significant. Learner Engagement had the strongest connection to Training Effectiveness with a correlation of .765, while Hybrid Learning and Trainer Competence were closely related to each other with a correlation of .818. Respondents usually gave similar ratings to different parts of the training.

### 5.3.3 Decision on the study hypothesis

There is a single overarching hypothesis regarding the three predictors considered together. The regression result is statistically significant at the 5 percent level. The null hypothesis is rejected, and the alternative hypothesis is supported for the combined model.

**Table 5.1: Decision on the overall regression hypothesis**

| Test | Result | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hybrid Learning, Trainer Competence, and Learner Engagement jointly predicting Training Effectiveness | *F*(3, 98) = 50.652; *p* < .001; *R*² = .608; valid N = 102 | Reject H₀; support H₁ for the combined model. |

The R² of .608 indicates that the predictors statistically explain 60.8 percent of the variation in effectiveness ratings across these cases. It is not a percentage related to learning or work performance that is caused by them.

### 5.3.4 Individual predictor results

A major overall model does not need three important individual coefficients. Table 5.2 illustrates which predictor maintains a unique relationship even after the other two variables are included in the regression analysis.

Learner Engagement had the clearest unique association. Hybrid learning and trainer competence showed positive relationships, but neither was individually statistically significant when all three factors were considered together. Both were still positively connected to Training Effectiveness, and they had a strong relationship with each other. Their practical importance cannot be ignored based solely on these coefficients.

Only the combined model makes a decision between H₀ and H₁. The three coefficient results explain the situation rather than serve as separate hypotheses.

## 5.4 Contribution of the Study

The contribution is a description from an employee about a hybrid programme at Union Bank. Union Vidya is included in the delivery setup, whereas the model independently assesses programme experience, trainer support, participation, and the perceived effectiveness. The existence of a platform on its own is not considered a result.

Much of the existing literature on hybrid learning focuses on education, whereas this survey addresses the application of hybrid learning within the context of banking work. Branch and office staff might engage in training related to deadlines, customer requirements, compliance responsibilities, and digital transformations. Their own involvement should be considered along with the design of the delivery process, as it can show circumstances that go beyond the platform or the trainer.

Breaking down the four experiences allows the results to be more detailed compared to a general opinion about whether employees enjoyed the training. All three predictors show a positive correlation with Training Effectiveness, but only Learner Engagement has a statistically significant coefficient in the overall model. The difference highlights the importance of preparation and involvement when a programme is assessed.

## 5.5 Suggestions for Training Review

Two outcomes establish the priorities: Learner Engagement is the only individually significant predictor in the combined regression, and digital preparation before face-to-face sessions is the lowest-rated Hybrid Learning item (Q6, mean = 3.990; Table 4.3). The suggestions listed are proposals that are meant for review, not yet tested interventions.

1. Make participation possible. A work-related scenario, example, inquiries, and short exercise can provide staff with methods to apply what they have heard. Programme reviewers can verify if employees are truly able to participate rather than just showing up.

2. Allow time for digital preparation. Coordinators can verify when staff have received the materials, login information, and a clear understanding of their task before the trainer-led session begins. This directly addresses the lower score given for question 6.

3. Show how the activities connect. The trainer may incorporate a banking task to build upon previously covered digital content, and then direct employees to the relevant resource they can review again later.

4. Fit training around branch work where possible. Customer service responsibilities and workload can take up time that could otherwise be used for preparation and active involvement. Local scheduling should consider the effect of that pressure.

5. Ask what needs attention. Short post-training feedback can help identify issues related to access, preparation, guidance, participation, and relevance. Staff members can then review the portion of the programme that employees found challenging.

**Table 5.3: How the findings inform the proposed training review**

| Evidence from this study | Area to review | Practical check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Learner Engagement is the only individually significant predictor in the combined model (*β* = .532, *p* < .001; N = 102). | Participation during training | Check whether employees have a realistic opportunity to ask, practise, and take part, rather than only attend or complete a module. |
| Digital preparation before the face-to-face session has the lowest Hybrid Learning item mean (Q6, 3.990; SPSS item N = 103). | Preparation before trainer-led learning | Check the timing, clarity, and work relevance of the material sent before a session. |
| Ten respondents were neutral about the trainer explaining the connection between modes (Q14; 102-case frequency table). | Connection between activities | Ask trainers to show explicitly how the earlier digital task relates to the live discussion or demonstration. |
| Eleven respondents were neutral about seeking clarification (Q22; 102-case frequency table). | Opportunities for questions | Check whether the programme gives employees a suitable way to ask questions in person or through the digital channel. |
| Eighty-eight respondents reported that training helped with relevant work tasks or problems (Q29; 102-case frequency table). | Application after training | Ask for specific examples of use and identify any support employees still need when they return to work. |

The table connects the reported patterns with areas that should be examined. The statement does not indicate that the suggested modifications were evaluated or that they would result in improved training results.

The first two rows provide the strongest support for review: engagement shows the most clearly defined adjusted link to effectiveness, and digital preparation has the lowest mean score among the Hybrid Learning items. The remaining rows convert item ratings into questions that need to be explored. Most of the responses remained positive, so a few neutral answers should lead to further questioning rather than concluding that there is a general failure.

## 5.6 Limitations of the Study

These findings are based on the intentionally chosen Chennai South respondents, not on a random selection of all Union Bank employees. One-time self-reports do not show the order of events or what caused what.

Each individual evaluated both the training experience and their perception of its results, which could enhance connections by using a common response approach. The SPSS output shows a single high alpha value for all 26 items, rather than four separate estimates for each construct. One case did not have a Trainer Competence mean and was removed from the main model using listwise deletion. The predictors also overlap a lot, especially Hybrid Learning and Trainer Competence; when looking at their individual coefficients, it is important to consider this overlap.

The paper and online forms used the same wording, but the differences in how responses were given were not tested. A formal response rate cannot be provided as the invitations and approaches were not fully accounted for. Supplementary 102-case item distributions are derived from scored records, while the main regression analysis is based on the saved SPSS viewer. Without the matching case file, it is not possible to confirm the exact agreement between the sources at the individual level. This restricts the extent to which all supplementary and SPSS outputs can be considered as a single, fully consistent dataset, even though the saved output includes the reported result of the stated hypothesis.

## 5.7 Directions for Future Research

Future efforts may involve expanding the analysis to include additional regions of Union Bank and comparing different programme types or job levels. Monitoring employees after they have completed training can provide evidence of how well the training is applied over time. Supervisor feedback or performance-related signs can help support employee evaluations.

Workload, manager support, learning time, digital confidence, and prior experience are also important factors that should be examined. One thing was not measured here, but each of them could still help explain why even when training is designed in the same way, not every employee ends up participating in the same manner.

A later survey could include recording invitations, keep track of response methods without gathering names, and use a single checked case file for each table. Checking in right after training and again once employees have had time to use it would give a clearer picture of how well the training was applied. That design would also be better suited for assessing a particular alteration in training methods.

## 5.8 Conclusion

The null hypothesis, that the predictors have no joint relationship with perceived Training Effectiveness, is rejected based on the 102 valid cases. The three predictors together have a significant relationship with perceived Training Effectiveness, as indicated by an R² of .608. The alternative hypothesis is supported by the combined model. When other factors are already considered, learner engagement still has a strong individual impact.

For Union Bank, the results suggest the need to examine their preparation and consider possibilities for active involvement. Digital resources and instructor-guided activities should be clearly related to the tasks that employees perform. These suggestions are based on the observed patterns; the analysis does not indicate that applying them would lead to improved results.

# Appendix B — Questionnaire Development and Literature Basis

The questionnaire was developed for employees to answer about one recent programme that combined digital and face-to-face learning. Tables B.1–B.4 show the concept addressed by each scored question, the literature that informed it, and the reason for its workplace wording. The cited papers support the question themes; the table does not assign an identical published scale item to every question. Questions 1–4 are eligibility and profile questions, so the tables begin with Question 5. Full references appear in the thesis reference list.

The three base papers have different roles in this development. Sosnova et al. (2025) support the broad connection between hybrid methods and learning effectiveness. Kim (2022) supplies corporate training evidence on delivery design, instructors, participation, and perceived outcomes. Bahl, Kiran and Sharma (2024) establish the relevance of training evaluation among Indian bank employees. The more specific item themes also draw on blended-learning environment, workplace learning, engagement, and training-transfer literature. Bahl’s paper is a banking-context source; it is not treated as an item bank for Hybrid Learning, Trainer Competence, or Learner Engagement.

**Table B.1: Literature basis for Hybrid Learning questions**

| Question and focus | Literature basis | Adaptation for this study |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Q5 — connection between digital and face-to-face parts | Garrison and Kanuka (2004); Graham (2006); Han and Ellis (2020) | Asks whether the respondent experienced one connected programme, rather than two unrelated activities. |
| Q6 — digital preparation before a face-to-face session | Han and Ellis (2020); Sosnova et al. (2025); Kim (2022) | Tests whether the earlier digital work had a clear preparatory role in the selected programme. |
| Q7 — clarification and application during face-to-face learning | Garrison and Kanuka (2004); Sosnova et al. (2025); Kim (2022) | Asks whether the trainer-led part helped the employee use or understand what was learned digitally. |
| Q8 — access to the programme’s digital platform | Mulaudzi (2021); Kumar and Pande (2017) | Refers to Union Vidya where used, or another authorised platform used for that programme. |
| Q9 — ease of navigating the platform | Mulaudzi (2021); Han and Ellis (2020) | Focuses on the employee’s ability to use the platform for learning; it is not a technical system audit. |
| Q10 — availability of digital learning material | Mulaudzi (2021); Han and Ellis (2020) | Asks whether required material was available when the employee needed it. |
| Q11 — access to material for later revision | Han and Ellis (2020); Mulaudzi (2021) | Captures whether the employee could return to the digital material after an initial activity. |

| Question and focus | Literature basis | Adaptation for this study |

| Q12 — trainer’s subject knowledge | Kim (2022); Ansari et al. (2023) | Asks about the trainer’s command of the topic in the selected programme. |
| Q13 — clarity of explanation | Kim (2022); Mulaudzi (2021) | Separates understandable explanation from simply making content available. |
| Q14 — explanation of the connection between modes | Kim (2022); Mulaudzi (2021) | Asks whether the trainer helped employees see how digital and face-to-face activities fitted together. |
| Q15 — effective use of digital tools | Kim (2022); Ansari et al. (2023) | Concerns the trainer’s use of tools during learning, not the platform’s technical performance. |

**Table B.4: Literature basis for Training Effectiveness questions**

| Question and focus | Literature basis | Adaptation for this study |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Q24 — understanding the main knowledge or skills | Kirkpatrick (1994); Kim (2022); Aziz (2015) | Measures employee-reported learning rather than satisfaction with attending. |

| Q29 — support for work tasks or problems | Aziz (2015); Bahl, Kiran and Sharma (2024); Blume et al. (2010) | Links reported learning to relevant tasks; the banking paper supports evaluation in this setting rather than the exact wording. |

The item map preserves the distinction between four constructs. Platform access and learning-mode integration belong to Hybrid Learning; trainer actions belong to Trainer Competence; the employee’s participation belongs to Learner Engagement; and reported learning and work application belong to Training Effectiveness. The pilot and the main-sample reliability results are discussed in Chapter 3. This appendix documents content development and does not by itself establish full validation of the instrument.
