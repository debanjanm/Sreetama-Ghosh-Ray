Hybrid Training and Effectiveness in Corporate Settings

Kim (2022) supplies the corporate training evidence. In South Korean automotive sales training, the way content is delivered has shifted to include traditional methods, fully digital formats, and hybrid approaches. The analysis includes 583 training instances from 2019 to 2020, consisting of 325 traditional sessions, 140 fully digital sessions, and 118 hybrid sessions. One key difference between the hybrid format and the more self-directed fully digital format is that the hybrid format includes both online and offline activities.

The training was assessed using the CIP-R framework developed by Kim, which encompasses content, instructional design, programmed learning, and recommendation.This framework focuses on aspects such as the content itself, the design of the instructional materials, personalized learning experiences, learners' perceived competence, and the effectiveness of recommendations provided. Reported effectiveness decreased when training transitioned from traditional methods to a fully digital format, but it increased again after the introduction of hybrid delivery. One of the factors linked to the reported outcome was how much the instructor interacted, the length of the session, the personalized learning experience, the level of confidence and ability gained, and the extent to which the skills were relevant to the job.

The sequence in which Kim's group organized their training matters: traditional training was considered the most effective, followed by hybrid training, and fully digital training was ranked as the least effective. The results showed that there was no significant statistical difference between the hybrid and fully digital delivery methods. Kim, therefore, cannot back up a broad statement that hybrid training is better than other methods. This paper is more helpful in demonstrating the importance of considering delivery design, instructor engagement, and practical application when assessing a corporate program.

Kim’s findings inform the present measurement choices. Training effectiveness goes beyond just a good feeling right away; hybrid learning focuses on how well the different methods work together; and trainer expertise stays important because having digital materials doesn’t take the place of personal guidance and feedback.

The term "hybrid" does not indicate whether an employee is able to access the materials, prepare adequately in advance, take part in the session, or have the opportunity to practice. These details are particularly important when staff members come from various branches while the usual work is still ongoing. For that reason, the Union Bank questionnaire includes questions about a program's related activities and the support that is available. It doesn't ask employees to compare formats they might never have used.

Kim compares corporate delivery arrangements. This thesis instead asks individual bank employees if a particular program helped them understand and apply work-related learning. The designs should not be expected to yield exactly the same outcomes, but the comparison at the corporate level makes it worthwhile to consider the question at the employee level.

In 2026, Uddin, Ahamed, Jakowan, Islam, and Nahar conducted a study involving 150 employees who had experience with blended learning. They looked into how the development of soft skills and the gain of knowledge relate to how well employees perform. Both factors related to learning showed positive connections with blended learning, but the direct effect of these factors on performance was not significant once the other connections were taken into account. Their result suggests that you shouldn't assume the outcome of work is determined just by how it is delivered.

Uddin et al.provide supporting workplace literature. Testing their specific soft skills, knowledge gain, and performance paths would need methods that are not included in this four-construct survey.

Trainer Competence in Hybrid Training

When transitioning from a platform-based training to an in-person session, the trainer assists employees in understanding the order of the training activities. One thing that can make a difference is having clear explanations of the subject, connecting the activities, using the right digital tools, asking questions, and getting feedback. Without this support, finishing an online task might not be properly linked to the employee's job duties.

The quality of the technical platform and the skill of the trainer are separate issues. Some materials might be found online without any information on why they're there; even an experienced trainer could find it difficult if employees can't access those materials. The questionnaire therefore includes separate sections on programme design and trainer support.

One thing that isn't included in this specific workplace model is the combination of Trainer Competence and Learner Engagement through base paper tests. Kim (2022) talks about the roles of instructors, the importance of participation, the value of practice, and the aspects of design in corporate training. Mulaudzi (2021) discusses the challenges related to facilitating and overcoming practical access barriers in the workplace. In a university context, Ansari and colleagues (2023) link blended-learning training to the competence and satisfaction of both lecturers and students. That population is different from bank employees, but the study provides support for looking at facilitation rather than considering technology availability as sufficient.

These sources assist in separating the trainer's input from the employee's reaction. The Trainer Competence areas include explaining concepts, using digital tools effectively, linking different modes of learning, helping participants engage, and giving useful feedback. The Learner Engagement elements include attention, effort, participation, asking questions, and showing interest. A good trainer rating does not necessarily mean that the learners are actively participating.

Learner Engagement

Engagement refers to being actively involved, which involves giving attention, putting in effort, taking part, showing interest, and seeking clarification when needed. A login or attendance record doesn't indicate if someone attempted to grasp a new process. Completion therefore offers an incomplete picture of the learning process.

Schaufeli, Bakker, and Salanova (2006) define work engagement as consisting of three key elements: vigour, dedication, and absorption. Their size does not indicate how much someone is involved in a single training program. The current items focus solely on the general concept of active effort: respondents share their level of attention, participation, involvement, questioning, and interest during the chosen programme.

Learner Engagement remains an independent factor because the design of the program and the skills of the trainer do not completely control how much attention or involvement an employee shows. Time pressure, past experience, confidence with digital tools, personal motivation, and the specific conditions of the branch can all influence the response. The model therefore looks at how engagement is connected to training effectiveness instead of assuming it follows a set order among the other factors and the result.

Training Effectiveness

Kirkpatrick’s framework divides the evaluation process into four stages: reaction, learning, behaviour, and results. That difference stops a good response to a session from representing all the results of training. An employee might experience a session without fully grasping its content, or grasp the content without later having the opportunity to use what they've learned.

Bahl, Kiran, and Sharma (2024) present a paper that serves as the foundation for the Indian banking sector. Their evaluation, which is open to all, includes 402 employees from both managerial and non-managerial roles across public, private, and foreign banks, and it is based on Kirkpatrick’s four levels. It highlights the importance of assessing training programs within the banking sector. The paper does not look at hybrid delivery methods, trainer skills, learner involvement, or Union Vidya, which is why it can't provide the current model on its own.
