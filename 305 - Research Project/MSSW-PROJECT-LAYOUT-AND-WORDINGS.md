# MSSW Project — How Past Students Actually Write (Wording Patterns & Mistakes to Avoid)

**Purpose of this document.** The same 8 department reference theses analyzed in [chapter-2/version-0/05-past-dissertation-summary.md](chapter-2/version-0/05-past-dissertation-summary.md) were re-read here with a different lens: not structure, sample sizes, or statistics, but **prose style** — how sentences are actually phrased, what boilerplate recurs almost word-for-word across independent students, and concrete grammar/clarity/wording mistakes Sreetama should not repeat. Where a mistake is both structural *and* a wording/honesty problem (e.g. a contradiction glossed over in prose), it is cross-referenced briefly rather than re-explained — go to the summary file for the statistical/methodological read of the same issue.

**Sources** (same 8 files, folder `references/`): Joanna Evangeline (2022, hybrid workplace/engagement), Sakthipriya R (2025, recognition/OCB, hospitality), Hari Kishore S (2017, TAFE-PSD training effectiveness), Ravina Narayanan (2021, digital workplace/hybrid), Bonfiglioli proposal (cross-functional collaboration/innovation), "Dressed to Perform" proposal (enclothed cognition), Palak Pandey (2024, women in leadership perception), Valli/HUL proposal (talent development/intention to stay). Full text extracted via `pdftotext -layout` and `textutil -convert txt`; all 8 read in full.

## Part 1 — Standard Wording Patterns by Section

### 1. Bonafide Certificate / Declaration (confirm once — near-identical everywhere)

Every full thesis in the set uses functionally the same two boilerplate blocks. Quoted once, from Sakthipriya R (2025):

> **Bonafide Certificate:** "This is to certify that the project titled '[TITLE]' is a bonafide project work done by Ms. [Name] (Register No- [xxx]), a second year student of M.A.HRM, Madras School of Social Work (Autonomous), Egmore, Chennai in partial fulfilment of the requirement for the Award of the Degree of Master of Arts in Human Resource Management and that the project has not been used previously for the award of any Degree, Diploma, Scholarship, Fellowship or any other project title."

> **Declaration:** "I, [NAME], final year student of M.A. HRM, hereby declare that the thesis entitled '[TITLE]' is the original work done by me under the guidance and supervision of [Guide], in partial fulfilment of the requirements for the award of the degree of Master of Arts in Human Resource Management, Madras School of Social work. I further declare that the research work has not been submitted at any other University or Institution, for the award of any degree or diploma or fellowship."

These are safe to copy near-verbatim (only names/titles/guide change) — every one of the 8 documents uses this exact skeleton.

### 2. Statement of the Problem — how they open/frame it

- Joanna (2022): *"In the context where neither extensive remote work nor fully on-site setups are perceived as the optimal workplace solutions after the Covid-19 pandemic, a hybrid approach may provide middle-ground... Hence, the purpose is to understand how hybrid workplace impacts employee engagement."*
- Sakthipriya (2025): *"Although recognition programs are increasingly seen as an effective means of encouraging and rewarding positive behavior, little is known about how they actually affect extra-role behavior in the hospitality industry... This gap raises a critical problem: to what extent do employee recognition programs foster extra-role behaviours, and how consistent are these effects across diverse hospitality settings?"*
- Hari Kishore (2017): *"The purpose of the study was to understand the effectiveness of behavioural training program conducted for the employees at TAFE – PSD LTD. The study aims at understanding the impact of training program on employees."*
- Palak Pandey (2024): *"The core issue that this research aims to address is the disparity between the growing number of women entering the workforce and the relatively low number who attain leadership positions."*

Pattern: 1–2 sentences naming the tension/gap in the field, then a sentence starting **"Hence,"** / **"This gap raises..."** / **"The core issue..."** that states the purpose directly. Ends by naming the IV→DV relationship in plain words, not yet formal hypothesis language.

### 3. Research Objectives — standard verb patterns

Consistently infinitive-verb openings, never questions:

- *"To study the implications of Hybrid work model on Employee Engagement."* (Joanna)
- *"To determine whether cross-functional collaboration has a significant positive impact on innovation at Bonfiglioli, Chennai."* (Bonfiglioli)
- *"To assess the level of cross-functional collaboration among employees..."* / *"To examine the relationship between cross-functional collaboration and innovation."* (Bonfiglioli, secondary objectives)
- *"To determine whether talent development practices have a significant positive influence on the intention of high-potential employees to stay..."* (Valli/HUL)
- *"To analyze the relationship between employee recognition programs and extra-role behaviour in the hospitality sector."* (Sakthipriya)
- *"To examine the influence of work attire on state self-esteem among professionals and interns across sectors."* (Dressed to Perform)

Verb rotation used across the corpus: **To study / To determine / To assess / To examine / To analyze / To identify / To evaluate**. Primary objective = the direct IV→DV test, stated first. Secondary objectives = sub-dimension breakdowns or descriptive levels of each variable, stated after.

### 4. Hypotheses — H0/H1 (or H0/Hα) phrasing conventions

Two conventions coexist in the department, both acceptable:

- **H0/H1 convention** (Joanna, Bonfiglioli, Valli/HUL, Ravina): *"H0: There exists no significant difference in Employee engagement levels between hybrid and Non-hybrid work models."* / *"H1: There exists a significant difference..."* — Bonfiglioli's cleaner variant: *"H₀: There is no significant impact of cross-functional collaboration on innovation... H₁: There is a significant positive impact of cross-functional collaboration on innovation..."*
- **H0/Hα convention** (Palak Pandey): *"H₀: Organisational culture does not significantly impact women's advancement to leadership positions. Hα: Organisational culture significantly impacts women's advancement to leadership positions."*

Consistent internal grammar: **H0 always negates with "no significant [difference/relationship/impact/influence]"**, and **H1/Hα always asserts the same noun positively**, usually adding **"positive"** when a directional effect is expected (e.g. *"a significant positive impact"*, *"a significant positive influence"*). Non-directional hypotheses are flagged explicitly as such (Dressed to Perform's H2 is noted in-text as *"Non-directional; this is the study's core originality claim"*).

### 5. Literature Review — introducing a study and transitioning between studies

Three distinct department conventions appear; any is acceptable, but pick one and use it consistently within a chapter (see Part 2, mistake examples of documents that drift between them mid-section):

**(a) Cite-then-summarize (Joanna):**
> *"The term employee engagement was initially coined by Kahn (1990) to describe the extent of the physical, cognitive, and emotional presence exhibited by people at work."*
> *"McKinsey conducted survey (Alexander et al., 2021) showed that 30% of employees would likely switch jobs if their organization would consider fully on-site work after the pandemic..."*

**(b) Summarize-then-cite, citation parenthetical at paragraph end (Sakthipriya):**
> *"This study examines how team-based recognition initiatives affect customer happiness, staff engagement, effort, and financial performance in business environments. The study, which lasted 12 weeks and was spread among six fast-food franchise sites, evaluated the impact of non-monetary team recognition... Research shows that staff involvement and effort are greatly increased by this kind of reward... (Presslee et al., 2022)"*

**(c) Summarize-then-explicit-relevance paragraph, named per source (Bonfiglioli, Valli — proposal documents):**
> *"Jassawalla and Sashittal examine how cross-functional new-product teams become genuinely collaborative... For the Bonfiglioli Chennai study, this paper supports measuring communication, shared goals, cooperation, trust, and commitment as dimensions of cross-functional collaboration."*
> *"...However, the present study differs by focusing specifically on Hindustan Unilever Limited, Chennai, excluding leadership development and affective commitment as separate variables."* (Valli, explicit gap-by-comparison closing sentence used after every reviewed paper)

Convention (c) — ending every literature paragraph with an explicit "how this differs from / supports the present study" sentence — is the single most rigorous transition habit in the set and worth adopting paper-by-paper in Sreetama's own Chapter 2, not only in a summary gap paragraph.

### 6. Methodology — standard phrasing for sampling, tools, limitations

- **Sampling technique** (near-identical wording, Joanna and Ravina): *"The study is based on Non – Probability Convenience Sampling. The researcher selected the accessible target group by selecting [companies/sector] and adopted convenience sampling method to achieve the sample size."*
- **Tools/instrument** phrasing: *"The researcher used 'Questionnaire' through Google forms as an instrument for collecting primary data, which proves to be less expensive and yet elaborative, when compared to other methods."* (Joanna, near-verbatim in Ravina too)
- **Reliability paragraph** (near-identical template, see Part 2 mistake #2 for the bug baked into it): *"Cronbach's alpha is commonly used measure of reliability for a set of two or more construct indicators. With values ranging from 0 to 1.0, the higher value indicates a higher reliability among the items... Although a commonly used benchmark for acceptable reliability is 0.60, lower values have been deemed acceptable when the research is descriptive in nature. The overall reliability value for this research is [X]."*
- **Limitations** phrasing: short bullet list, each one sentence, hedged: *"The research takes into account a sample size that may not be entirely reflective of the entire population."* (Joanna); *"Responses depend on employees' perceptions (may include bias)"* (Sakthipriya); *"Sample size restricted to 100–150 employees, may not represent entire industry."* (Sakthipriya).

### 7. Findings/Interpretation — phrasing a significant vs. non-significant result in prose

**Significant result:**
> *"A Pearson correlation was conducted to examine the relationship... The results indicate a strong positive correlation... r = .837, n = 129, p < .001. Since the p-value is less than 0.05, the result is statistically significant... Therefore, the null hypothesis (H₀₁)... is rejected, and the alternative hypothesis (H₁₁) is accepted."* (Sakthipriya)
> *"From the above table it can be inferred that the t statistics probability value for Infrastructure is < .001 which is less than 0.05 level of significance, hence we reject the null hypothesis. Thus, it can be concluded that the infrastructure provided in a hybrid working environment influences the communication in the digital workplace."* (Ravina)

**Non-significant result:**
> *"Since p > 0.05, the null hypothesis (H₀₃) is accepted. This means gender does not significantly impact the relationship between employee recognition and extra-role behaviour."* (Sakthipriya)
> *"...the probability value 0.739 is more than the alpha value 0.05, therefore we accept the H0. Hence, we conclude that there is no significant difference between the age groups and communication aspect of digital workplace management."* (Ravina)

Consistent skeleton: **[test name] → numeric result stated with comparison operator against 0.05 → "reject/accept the null hypothesis" → one plain-English sentence restating what that means for the variables named in the objective.** Descriptive-table inferences follow a simpler formula: *"It is inferred from table X that [Y]% of the respondents [response]..."* (Joanna) or *"The above table shows that [Y]% of respondents agree and [Z]% strongly agree that..."* (Sakthipriya) — always percentage-led, always tied back to the exact table number.

### 8. Conclusion/Recommendations — standard closing phrasing patterns

- *"The study highlights the critical role of employee recognition in shaping extra-role behaviour within the hospitality sector. Findings revealed a strong and significant link... Overall, the results emphasize that recognition is not just a symbolic gesture but a strategic tool for..."* (Sakthipriya)
- *"Optimizing the digital workplace with integrated technology and fully supported staff is the key to a successful hybrid work culture."* (Ravina)
- *"The research provides a comprehensive understanding of the perceptions of women in leadership roles and the barriers they face... It reveals that while there have been improvements... challenges such as gender bias, work-life balance issues, and insufficient mentorship continue to impede women's progress."* (Palak)

Pattern: open with **"The study/research reveals/highlights/provides..."**, restate the strongest 1–2 findings in plain language, then a closing sentence that reframes the topic as practically/strategically important ("...is not just X, but a strategic tool for Y"). Recommendations are usually a numbered or bulleted list, each item a short imperative ("Establish...", "Design...", "Adopt...") followed by 1 sentence of elaboration — see Sakthipriya's 7-point Suggestions list as the cleanest example.

### 9. Acknowledgements (brief note — low priority)

All four full theses open with the nearly identical line *"First and foremost, I thank God for his immense blessings and making this study happen as planned,"* followed by thanking the Principal (Dr. Raja Samuel) by name, then the HOD, then the guide, then respondents. Safe to follow this order, but see Part 2 mistake #1 — do not blind-copy a template acknowledgement without checking every pronoun and name against your own guide.

## Part 2 — Mistakes to Avoid (Wording / Clarity, Not Statistics)

| # | Thesis | Quoted problem | What's wrong | Better version |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hari Kishore (2017), Acknowledgement | *"I would like to thank my guide, Mr Hemakumar for helping me constantly with all **her** inputs/feedback..."* | Pronoun-gender mismatch — Mr. Hemakumar is male, but the sentence uses "her." Strong evidence of an unedited copy-paste from a template written for a female guide. | *"...for helping me constantly with all **his** inputs/feedback..."* — always re-read every pronoun in copied boilerplate against the actual person. |
| 2 | Joanna (2022) & Ravina (2021), Reliability section | *"Cronbach's alpha is used in this research to **access** the scale's internal consistency in terms of how well the items measured the same construct or dimension of **perceived training quality**."* | Two bugs, both copy-pasted verbatim between two unrelated theses (one is about hybrid work/engagement, the other about digital workplace management — **neither is about training**): (a) "access" should be "assess"; (b) "perceived training quality" is a leftover phrase from whatever source this boilerplate was originally drafted for. Proves the paragraph was block-copied without being read. | *"...to **assess** the scale's internal consistency in terms of how well the items measured the same construct — [name your actual construct, e.g. employee engagement]."* Always read boilerplate you reuse; never leave a construct name in a reliability paragraph that isn't your own. |
| 3 | Hari Kishore (2017), Limitations | *"The sample size is small so the result **may be generalized**."* | Says the opposite of the intended meaning — a small sample is a reason results can*not* be generalized. Almost certainly a missing "not." | *"The sample size is small, so the result may **not** be generalized [beyond this sample]."* |
| 4 | Hari Kishore (2017), Conclusion (Ch. 5) | *"...the training program provides an opportunity to the employee to **boom in their life**... hence the productivity of the company increases to a great extent."* | Vague/unclear idiom ("boom in their life") plus an overclaim: Chapter 4's own chi-square test found **no significant relationship** between training and performance (p = .924, null accepted), yet the conclusion asserts productivity "increases to a great extent" without qualification. This is the wording-level version of the honesty gap the structural summary already flags — the fix here is specifically how the sentence is phrased, not just that the finding is unreconciled. | *"...gives employees an opportunity to develop professionally, though this study's own inferential test did not find a statistically significant link between training and performance — a result the descriptive satisfaction scores do not fully explain."* Say what the data showed, including the null result, in the same sentence as the positive descriptive finding. |
| 5 | Palak Pandey (2024), Ch. 4 Inference (Objective 3/Hypothesis 4) | *"...the p-value of the variable team performance, morale, conflict resolution, and creativity are 0.565, 0.231, 0.306, 0.523 respectively which is **less than 0.05**... Hence, null hypothesis for these variables is accepted."* | Direct self-contradiction in one sentence: none of those values are less than 0.05 (all are greater), and "null hypothesis accepted" is only correct because p > 0.05. Chapter 5's own restatement of the same finding later gets it right ("Since all p-values are **greater than** 0.05..."), which makes this an internal inconsistency, not just a typo. | *"...which is **greater than** 0.05, at the 5% level of significance. Hence, the null hypothesis for these variables is accepted."* |
| 6 | Sakthipriya (2025), Ch. 4 Inference (Objective 1) | *"...the alternative hypothesis (H₁₁) is accepted. **Hence when employee is recognised, he/she exhibits extra role behaviour** in the organisation."* | Causal language from a correlational test. The preceding analysis is a Pearson correlation (r = .837) — it shows association, not that recognition *causes* extra-role behaviour. The final sentence states a direct causal mechanism the design cannot support. | *"...is associated with higher levels of extra-role behaviour."* (Keep the causal framing only for the regression's R² discussion, and even there qualify it — a cross-sectional survey cannot establish causation.) |
| 7 | Sakthipriya (2025), Literature Review | *"...this study investigates the connection between optimistic thinking and extra-role actions. **conducted using a census approach** among 166 workers of the Shiraz Province's General Directorate of Roads."* | Sentence fragment starting with a lowercase verb after a full stop — reads as if a clause was accidentally split into two "sentences." | Merge into one sentence: *"...this study investigates the connection between optimistic thinking and extra-role actions, conducted using a census approach among 166 workers..."* |
| 8 | Sakthipriya (2025), Ch. 3 Hypotheses (Objectives 3 & 4) | *"H₀₄: Gender **does not significantly differ** the relationship with extra role behaviour."* / *"H₁₄: Gender **significantly differ** the relationship..."* | "Differ" is used as a transitive verb ("differ the relationship") — not standard English — and Objective 3 and Objective 4 are worded so similarly (both testing "gender... the relationship...") that a reader cannot tell them apart without re-reading Chapter 4's tables. | *"H₀: Gender does not significantly **affect** the relationship between employee recognition and extra-role behaviour."* Keep each hypothesis's wording distinct enough that the DV being tested is unambiguous from the sentence alone. |
| 9 | Ravina (2021), Ch. 4 Inference (Hypothesis 3, Team Management) | *"...the probability value 0.050 is **equal the** alpha value 0.05, therefore we accept the H0."* | Missing "to" ("equal to the alpha value"). Also treats a boundary case (p = .050, exactly at the cutoff) as a routine accept-H0 without comment — a wording choice that hides how close this result is to significant (cf. the structural summary's note on this same borderline case). | *"...the probability value 0.050 is equal **to** the alpha value 0.05; since it does not fall below the threshold, we accept H0, though this result sits at the boundary of significance and should be interpreted cautiously."* |
| 10 | Ravina (2021), Literature Review | *"Hybrid gatherings, in which **co-found and far off members** associate through video or/sound, have become universal... directing **half breed gatherings** isn't clear... Utilizing an interdisciplinary methodology that draws from **strength hypothesis in socioecology**..."* | The most serious prose problem in the corpus: several literature-review paragraphs read as machine-paraphrased ("spun") text — "half breed" for "hybrid," "co-found" for "co-located," "far off" for "remote/distant," "strength hypothesis in socioecology" for something like "resilience theory in socio-ecology." The result is close to unreadable and would not survive a supervisor's read. | Never run source material through an automatic paraphrasing/synonym-swap tool. Summarize a source in your own words instead — it is slower but produces readable, defensible prose, and paraphrasing tools like this one actively damage academic credibility. |
| 11 | Hari Kishore (2017), Ch. 1 Industry Profile | *"...it looks like **old is gold** for the battery industry as the old technology is yet to be disrupted..."* | Casual idiom dropped into otherwise formal academic prose — register mismatch. | *"...lead-acid battery technology remains cost-competitive and has yet to be displaced by newer chemistries."* |
| 12 | Joanna (2022), Ch. 1 (sourced from an uncited web article) | *"**In our business**, mutual appreciation and positive assumptions are guiding principles. When we don't see each other every day, it's easy to make assumptions about other employees - **we** always hold positive assumptions..."* | First-person plural ("our business," "we") breaks the third-person academic register used everywhere else in the chapter — a clear sign of an unedited copy-paste from a first-person source (a company blog or Forbes-style article) rather than a paraphrase. | Rewrite in third person and attribute: *"Organizations that adopt a norm of positive assumptions about colleagues, even without daily face-to-face contact, report stronger workplace trust (Source, Year)."* |
| 13 | Joanna (2022), Table 4.13 Inference | *"...32% of the respondents feel that working from office is very important and 58% ... somewhat important whereas **32%** of the respondents feel that working from the office is **not at all important**."* | The table itself shows "Not at all Important" = 7 respondents = ~8%, not 32%. The 32% figure has been copy-pasted from the "Very Important" row into the "Not at all Important" sentence — a proofreading slip that materially misreports the data in prose even though the table above it is correct. | Always re-check every percentage quoted in an inference sentence against the table it summarizes, especially when copy-pasting the sentence structure from a previous inference paragraph. |
| 14 | Palak Pandey (2024), Ch. 3 Chapterisation | *"Chapter 3: ...This chapter **will outline** the research design, data collection methods (**e.g., surveys and interviews**)..."* | Two issues: (a) the whole chapterisation section is written in future tense ("will outline," "will discuss," "will conclude") even though this is a completed, submitted thesis — inconsistent with Joanna's and Sakthipriya's present-tense chapterisation ("the third chapter deals with..."); (b) "interviews" is listed as a data collection method, but the actual study used only a self-constructed questionnaire — a leftover from an earlier proposal draft never updated to match what was actually done. | Use present tense for a submitted document ("This chapter outlines...") and list only the methods actually used ("...data collection via a structured questionnaire..."). |
| 15 | Citation format (Joanna, 2022) | *"(Michal Beno,2021)"*, *"(Anja Bojic,2022)"* vs. elsewhere *"(Cook et al., 2020)"* | Inconsistent citation format within one document: some citations use full first name with no space after the comma; others use the standard surname + space + year convention. | Pick one format (surname, year — the department norm) and apply it to every citation, including ones drawn from blogs/magazines. |

## Part 3 — "Do This, Not That" Quick Reference

| Section | Do this | Not that |
|---|---|---|
| Objectives | Start with an infinitive verb: *To examine / To assess / To determine / To analyze / To study* | A question ("How does X affect Y?") or a noun phrase with no verb |
| Hypotheses | State **H0 first**, always negating with "no significant [difference/relationship/impact/influence]"; then **H1/Hα**, asserting the same noun, adding "positive" only if the effect is directional | Skipping H0 and only writing the alternative; using "differ" as a transitive verb ("gender differ the relationship") |
| Hypothesis wording ↔ what's tested | Make sure the noun in your hypothesis matches the exact variable/table you test in Chapter 4 (e.g. if H2 says "leadership opportunities," don't test "advancement to leadership positions" instead) | Letting the hypothesis and the actual statistical test drift into near-synonyms |
| Literature review | Pick ONE convention — cite-then-summarize, summarize-then-cite, or summarize-then-explicit-relevance — and use it for every source in a chapter. End each paragraph with a sentence on how the source supports or differs from your own model | Mixing conventions paragraph to paragraph; ending a lit-review paragraph with nothing tying it back to your model |
| Paraphrasing sources | Read the source, then write the summary in your own words | Running text through an automatic paraphrasing/synonym-spinning tool (see Part 2 #10 — it produces unreadable prose like "half breed gatherings") |
| Reliability paragraph | Reuse the standard Cronbach's alpha paragraph, but **read every word** before submitting — check "assess" not "access," and that the construct named is actually yours | Copy-pasting a reliability paragraph from another thesis without updating the construct name |
| Findings prose (significant) | "[Test] was conducted... [statistic], p [operator] .05. Since p [operator] 0.05, the null hypothesis is [rejected/accepted]. [One sentence in plain English on what this means for the named variables]." | Reporting the p-value's direction incorrectly relative to the accept/reject conclusion (Part 2 #5) |
| Causal language | Use "is associated with" / "predicts" (for regression) | "Hence when X happens, Y happens" — stated as direct causation from a correlation or a cross-sectional survey |
| Register/person | Third person, present tense, throughout — including the chapterisation section of a completed thesis | First person ("I feel that...", "In our business...") or future tense ("This chapter will...") in a submitted document |
| Register/tone | Formal register even when a source is casual | Idioms/colloquialisms carried over from a source ("old is gold") |
| Boilerplate (ack., bonafide, sampling-technique, reliability paragraphs) | Reuse the department's standard phrasing — it's safe and expected — but re-read every name, pronoun, and construct after pasting | Blind copy-paste that leaves a wrong pronoun (Part 2 #1) or a mismatched construct name (Part 2 #2) in place |
| Percentages/numbers in prose | Re-check every number quoted in an inference sentence against the table above it | Copy-pasting the previous inference sentence's structure and forgetting to update the number (Part 2 #13) |
| Honesty about mixed results | If a descriptive result is positive but the inferential test is null (or vice versa), say so explicitly in the same paragraph | Letting the conclusion chapter quietly drop the null result and assert success anyway (Part 2 #4) |
| Citations | Surname + comma + space + year, consistently: (Author, Year) | Full name, no space: (First Last,Year) |
