# Hybrid Learning Literature Matrix

| Source | Setting and evidence | Questionnaire or measurement status | Use in Version 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Garrison and Kanuka (2004) | Conceptual paper on the purposeful integration of online and face-to-face learning. | No questionnaire. | Defines hybrid learning and explains why simply adding online material is not enough. |
| Kumar and Pande (2017) | Working-professional and technology-mediated learning context. Identifies institutional, facilitator, learner, and pedagogy conditions. | No validated ready-made scale. | Supports integration, access, flexibility, digital resources, and trainer guidance as Hybrid Learning dimensions. |
| Han and Ellis (2020) | Validated blended-learning-environment study in higher education. | Perceptions of the Blended Learning Environment Questionnaire; dimensions include integration, online contribution, and online workload. | Supports the structure of the Hybrid Learning construct. Item wording is adapted for employee training, not copied as a corporate scale. |
| Mulaudzi (2021) | South African workplace training in the transport sector. | Structured employee questionnaire; not a validated journal scale. | Practical corporate wording for connectivity, LMS access, time management, and employee experience. |
| Vo, Zhu and Diep (2017) | Meta-analysis of 51 effect sizes from higher-education courses; small positive average effect on student performance (*g* = 0.385). | Outcome synthesis; no questionnaire source. | Background evidence that thoughtfully blended delivery can be associated with learning performance. It is not corporate or banking evidence and does not justify a direct claim about Union Bank employees. |
| Yu, Yu, Li and Wang (2025) | Meta-analysis of 133 empirical student studies involving 18,464 participants; upper-medium effect on student learning performance (*g* = 0.651). | Outcome synthesis; no questionnaire source. | Background evidence for integrated learning design only. The student setting and varied educational designs mean it is not used as direct workplace-effectiveness evidence. |
| Uddin et al. (2026) | Corporate employees with prior blended-training experience. | Uses a blended-learning-to-performance model; the published paper is not treated as a complete respondent-ready questionnaire source. | Supports the central workplace relationship between blended learning and employee outcomes. |
| Union Bank of India (2024, 2025) | Official annual-report account of the Bank’s learning system. The reports identify Union Vidya as the LMS and describe digital resources, e-learning, virtual and staggered programmes, role-based training, and the Training Management System. | Not a research instrument. | Establishes the local digital-learning and trainer-led context. It does not support a claim that every programme is hybrid or that all employees use identical Union Vidya features. |

## Hybrid Learning Dimensions Used for Item Design

1. **Integration:** whether digital and in-person parts build on one another.
2. **LMS and access:** ease of access, usability, reliability, and availability of learning material on the digital platform used for the programme.
3. **Flexibility and digital content:** ability to learn at a suitable time, revisit material, and prepare for later sessions.
4. **Interaction and facilitation:** opportunities to ask questions, receive feedback, and participate with the trainer across both modes.

These are dimensions of the one Hybrid Learning variable. They are not separate independent variables or separate hypotheses.
