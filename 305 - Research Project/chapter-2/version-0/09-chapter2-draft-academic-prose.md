## 2.1 Overview of Relevant Review

### Conceptualizing hybrid and blended learning

Hybrid or blended learning is most commonly defined as the deliberate integration of digital and face-to-face instructional modes rather than their mere co-occurrence. Garrison and Kanuka (2004) offered the most widely cited formulation of this distinction, describing blended learning as the "thoughtful integration" of classroom and online experience, a definition that explicitly separates genuine blended design from the simple addition of an online component to an unchanged classroom programme. Bonk and Graham (2006), in their edited handbook on blended learning, extended this definitional work across a range of organizational and educational contexts, including workplace training. Boelens, De Wever and Voet (2017), following a systematic screening of 640 sources, identified four recurring design challenges in blended learning implementation: flexibility, interaction, facilitation of the learning process, and the affective learning climate. Their review supports the position that blended learning's effectiveness is a function of design quality and integration rather than delivery mode alone, a position consistent with the framing adopted in this study.

### Hybrid learning in workplace and organizational training

While blended learning has been studied extensively in higher education, its application to organizational training remains comparatively underdeveloped. Bin Mubayrik (2018), in a systematic review of workplace blended learning covering seventeen studies and approximately 3,250 participants, concluded that blended approaches are particularly well suited to workplace settings because of their capacity to support authentic and collaborative learning, while also noting that the evidence base for workplace contexts remains considerably thinner than for higher education. Ashraf et al. (2021), in an umbrella review synthesizing fifty-seven systematic reviews of blended learning, similarly found that the field has concentrated overwhelmingly on student populations, with engagement, self-regulation, and satisfaction emerging as the most frequently studied psychological outcomes.

Two recent studies offer more direct precedent for workplace application. Agarwal, Goyal, Kumar and Modi (2026) examined a hybrid employee-development intervention combining instructor-led training, self-paced e-learning, gamified simulation, and on-the-job practice within the Indian financial-services sector, tracking 458 employees longitudinally and finding significant post-intervention performance gains; the authors identify the specific mechanisms and conditions underlying such gains as an area requiring further research. Lee (2010) examined a six-week blended leadership-development programme at a corporate university in Korea, with particular attention to the transfer of learning into the workplace, and found that different design elements — online demonstration and activation, offline application — mattered at different stages of the transfer process. Lee's findings support a central premise of the present study: that training effectiveness cannot be reduced to immediate learning outcomes but must be understood as a chain extending through retention and application into workplace performance.

### Evidence on blended learning effectiveness

Meta-analytic evidence on blended learning's effectiveness relative to conventional instruction is mixed but generally favourable when design quality is accounted for. Bernard, Borokhovski, Schmid, Tamim and Abrami (2014), synthesizing 117 studies, reported a small-to-medium positive effect of blended over traditional instruction, with larger effects in STEM disciplines. A related meta-analysis (Bernard et al., 2017) similarly found a positive mean effect at course level, while cautioning that effect sizes vary considerably by discipline and design, suggesting that the presence of a blend is not by itself sufficient to guarantee superior outcomes. A separate systematic review found that replacing between 30 and 79 percent of classroom time with online instruction produced learning outcomes broadly equivalent to, rather than systematically superior to, conventional instruction. Taken together, this literature reinforces the conclusion that blend ratio alone is a poor predictor of effectiveness, and that the mechanisms through which a blend is designed and experienced by learners deserve closer empirical attention.

### Learner engagement as an explanatory mechanism

The most direct precedent for the present study is Saroj, Sahney and Sekar (2026), who examined workplace blended-learning training among 375 employees and modelled learner engagement as a mediator between blended-learning acceptance, instructor competence, and metacognitive skills on one hand, and employee agility on the other, with supervisor support tested as a moderator of the engagement–agility relationship. Their structural equation modelling analysis found that acceptance, instructor competence, and metacognitive skills each positively predicted engagement, that engagement in turn predicted employee agility, and that supervisor support strengthened this latter relationship. This study is treated as the base paper for the present research (see Section 2.2), and its engagement-mediation logic underpins the conceptual model developed here.

Related evidence on engagement's antecedents comes from Ye, Kuang and Liu (2022), who examined 562 teachers using PLS-SEM and found that ICT self-efficacy and organizational support for blended learning predicted attitudes toward blended learning, which in turn predicted its use, jointly explaining 54.7 percent of variance in usage; their findings position technology readiness as an antecedent condition rather than the central explanatory mechanism, a distinction reflected in the present study's decision to treat technology-related items as a dimension of the hybrid-learning construct rather than a separate mediating variable. Yang (2023) similarly modelled a chain from organizational support through ICT self-efficacy and engagement to satisfaction among 245 participants, again supporting engagement's centrality as a psychological mechanism in blended-learning outcomes.

### Knowledge retention in blended training

Retention of training content beyond the immediate post-training period is comparatively under-measured in the blended-learning literature, which tends to privilege immediate learning outcomes over longer-term recall. Wakekar et al. (2026), in a comparative study of 248 community health workers assigned to blended, traditional, and e-learning-only training, measured knowledge retention at six months and found substantially higher retention in the blended group (78.2 percent) than in the traditional (62.4 percent) or e-learning-only (58.7 percent) groups. This finding provides empirical support for treating retention as a distinct downstream outcome of blended training rather than folding it into a single effectiveness measure, though it is notable that Wakekar et al. operationalized retention as an objective recall-test score rather than a multi-item self-report perception scale of the kind adopted in this study; this distinction is addressed further in Chapter 3.

### Training transfer and job performance

The final stage of the outcome chain examined in this study — the translation of training into workplace performance — draws on a longer-established literature on training transfer. Baldwin and Ford's (1988) foundational review identified trainee characteristics, training design, and the work environment as the three principal determinants of transfer, a framework subsequently updated by Blume, Ford, Baldwin and Huang (2010) through meta-analysis, which confirmed the continuing importance of work-environment and climate factors as moderators of transfer. Facteau, Dobbins, Russell, Ladd and Kudisch (1995) extended this line of enquiry by demonstrating that organizational commitment and multiple sources of social support predict pre-training motivation, which in turn predicts perceived transfer, establishing an early empirical link between organizational support and training outcomes via a motivational mechanism. For the measurement of job performance itself, this study draws on the task-performance and contextual-performance distinction established by Williams and Anderson (1991) and later operationalized in the Individual Work Performance Questionnaire (Koopmans et al., 2011, 2014), an instrument validated across occupational sectors and multiple national contexts.

### Training effectiveness in the Indian banking sector

Research specifically addressing training effectiveness within Indian banking remains limited. Jain and Jain (2015) examined training effectiveness across public, private, and foreign banks operating in India using a five-dimension construct — commitment to training, access, relevance, benefits, and satisfaction — and found no significant variation in training effectiveness by bank ownership type. A more recent study applied Kirkpatrick's four-level evaluation model to a sample of 402 managerial and non-managerial employees across Indian banks, reporting that the four levels were interlinked and that the model was effective for evaluating training's impact on motivation and bank performance; this study is one of the few to apply Kirkpatrick's framework within the Indian banking context specifically, though its findings speak to the model's descriptive applicability rather than to the explanatory mechanisms examined in the present research.

## 2.2 Theoretical Framework

This study draws on five bodies of theory to construct its conceptual model.

Kirkpatrick's (1994) four-level model of training evaluation — reaction, learning, behaviour, and results — provides the overarching logic for the outcome chain examined in this study, in which training effectiveness, knowledge retention, and job performance are treated as successive stages rather than a single undifferentiated outcome. Holton (1996) offered an influential critique and extension of Kirkpatrick's model, arguing that ability, motivation, and environmental factors jointly determine learning, individual performance, and organizational results; this critique provides theoretical justification for including learner engagement and, in an extended model, trainer competence as explanatory mechanisms rather than treating training delivery mode as directly predictive of outcomes.

Fredricks, Blumenfeld and Paris (2004) established the behavioural, emotional, and cognitive components of engagement that underpin most subsequent engagement scales, including the workplace-training-specific measures referenced in Section 2.1. Davis's (1989) Technology Acceptance Model, and the computer self-efficacy construct developed by Compeau and Higgins (1995), provide the theoretical basis for the technology-readiness items incorporated into the hybrid-learning construct in this study. Ryan and Deci's (2000) self-determination theory, centred on autonomy, competence, and relatedness, offers a theoretical account of why flexible, learner-controlled blended formats may support motivation, though motivation is not modelled as a separate construct in the present study for reasons discussed in Chapter 3. Finally, Garrison and Kanuka's (2004) definition of blended learning as thoughtful integration, rather than mere combination, of online and face-to-face modes directly informs the Integration dimension of the hybrid-learning construct measured in this study.

Synthesizing these frameworks, the present study proposes that hybrid learning influences training effectiveness through the mechanism of learner engagement, itself shaped by trainer competence as a parallel antecedent, and that training effectiveness in turn predicts knowledge retention and, ultimately, job performance — a five-construct model referred to throughout this dissertation as Model-Alpha.

## 2.4 Gaps in the Study

Four gaps in the existing literature inform the present research design.

First, an explanatory gap: the literature reviewed above establishes that blended learning is generally associated with positive outcomes, but comparatively few studies examine the psychological or organizational mechanisms through which this association arises. Ashraf et al.'s (2021) umbrella review of fifty-seven systematic reviews found engagement, satisfaction, and self-regulation studied primarily as outcomes rather than as tested explanatory mechanisms within a formal model — a limitation this study addresses by modelling engagement as a mediator rather than an endpoint.

Second, a population gap: the same literature is concentrated overwhelmingly in higher-education and student samples. Bin Mubayrik's (2018) review of workplace blended learning identified only seventeen qualifying studies against a substantially larger higher-education literature, indicating that findings from student populations cannot be assumed to generalize to organizational training contexts.

Third, a geographic and sectoral gap: very few studies address blended or hybrid learning specifically within Indian public-sector banking. Jain and Jain (2015) and the more recent Kirkpatrick-based Indian-banking study (Section 2.1) represent the closest available precedents, but neither examines hybrid learning as a construct in the sense developed in this study.

Fourth, a chain-depth gap: no study identified in this review tests a full sequential chain from hybrid learning through engagement, training effectiveness, knowledge retention, and job performance within a single model. Saroj, Sahney and Sekar (2026) test engagement as a mediator but conclude at employee agility rather than extending to retention or performance; Wakekar et al. (2026) measure retention directly but do not model engagement or a subsequent performance stage.

Taken together, these four gaps support the following statement of the research problem: while substantial literature confirms that blended and hybrid learning are associated with improved engagement, satisfaction, and learning outcomes, this evidence is concentrated in higher education and does not adequately explain the psychological mechanisms through which hybrid learning contributes to workplace training effectiveness, nor has it been examined within the specific context of Indian public-sector banking, nor has any study yet tested a complete outcome chain extending from engagement through to job performance. The present study addresses these four gaps concurrently by examining a hybrid-learning model, mediated by learner engagement, among employees of Union Bank of India.
