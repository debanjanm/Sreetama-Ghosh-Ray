Chapter 2 — Review of Literature

## 2.1 Overview of Relevant Review

### Conceptualizing hybrid and blended learning

Hybrid or blended learning is most commonly defined as the deliberate integration of digital and face-to-face instructional modes rather than their mere co-occurrence. Garrison and Kanuka (2004) offered the most widely cited formulation of this distinction, describing blended learning as the "thoughtful integration" of classroom and online experience, a definition that explicitly separates genuine blended design from the simple addition of an online component to an unchanged classroom programme. Bonk and Graham (2006), in their edited handbook on blended learning, extended this definitional work across a range of organizational and educational contexts, including workplace training. Boelens, De Wever and Voet (2017), following a systematic screening of 640 sources, identified four recurring design challenges in blended learning implementation: flexibility, interaction, facilitation of the learning process, and the affective learning climate. Their review supports the position that blended learning's effectiveness is a function of design quality and integration rather than delivery mode alone, a position consistent with the framing adopted in this study. Han and Ellis (2020) approached this same definitional problem instrumentally, developing the Perceptions of the Blended Learning Environment Questionnaire around a general perception scale together with three subscales addressing distinct aspects of the online component specifically, rather than treating the online and face-to-face components as a single undifferentiated experience. This general-plus-subscale structure offers a useful template for the present study's own hybrid-learning construct, which is similarly organized into distinct dimensions rather than a single global item.

### Hybrid learning in workplace and organizational training

While blended learning has been studied extensively in higher education, its application to organizational training remains comparatively underdeveloped. Bin Mubayrik (2018), in a systematic review of workplace blended learning covering seventeen studies and approximately 3,250 participants, concluded that blended approaches are particularly well suited to workplace settings because of their capacity to support authentic and collaborative learning, while also noting that the evidence base for workplace contexts remains considerably thinner than for higher education. Ashraf et al. (2021), in an umbrella review synthesizing fifty-seven systematic reviews of blended learning, similarly found that the field has concentrated overwhelmingly on student populations, with engagement, self-regulation, and satisfaction emerging as the most frequently studied psychological outcomes.

Two recent studies offer more direct precedent for workplace application. Agarwal, Goyal, Kumar and Modi (2026) examined a hybrid employee-development intervention combining instructor-led training, self-paced e-learning, gamified simulation, and on-the-job practice within the Indian financial-services sector, tracking 458 employees longitudinally and finding significant post-intervention performance gains; the authors identify the specific mechanisms and conditions underlying such gains as an area requiring further research. Lee (2010) examined a six-week blended leadership-development programme at a corporate university in Korea, with particular attention to the transfer of learning into the workplace, and found that different design elements — online demonstration and activation, offline application — mattered at different stages of the transfer process. Lee's findings support a central premise of the present study: that training effectiveness cannot be reduced to immediate learning outcomes but must be understood as a chain extending through retention and application into workplace performance.

### Evidence on blended learning effectiveness

Meta-analytic evidence on blended learning's effectiveness relative to conventional instruction is mixed but generally favourable when design quality is accounted for. Bernard, Borokhovski, Schmid, Tamim and Abrami (2014), synthesizing 117 studies, reported a small-to-medium positive effect of blended over traditional instruction, with larger effects in STEM disciplines. A related meta-analysis (Bernard et al., 2017) similarly found a positive mean effect at course level, while cautioning that effect sizes vary considerably by discipline and design, suggesting that the presence of a blend is not by itself sufficient to guarantee superior outcomes. A separate systematic review found that replacing between 30 and 79 percent of classroom time with online instruction produced learning outcomes broadly equivalent to, rather than systematically superior to, conventional instruction. Taken together, this literature reinforces the conclusion that blend ratio alone is a poor predictor of effectiveness, and that the mechanisms through which a blend is designed and experienced by learners deserve closer empirical attention. Effectiveness in this literature is also not measured uniformly: Wang (2003), developing the Learner Satisfaction Questionnaire for asynchronous e-learning, treated satisfaction itself as a multi-dimensional outcome spanning perceived usefulness, course quality, system quality, instructor quality, and service quality, rather than as a single global rating. This multi-dimensional treatment of satisfaction is a reminder that "training effectiveness," as used loosely across much of the literature reviewed above, is itself a construct assembled from several distinguishable components, a point the present study addresses directly by anchoring its own Training Effectiveness measure to Kirkpatrick's levels rather than to satisfaction alone.

### Learner engagement as an explanatory mechanism

The present study's base paper is Ye, Kuang and Liu (2022), who examined 562 teachers using PLS-SEM and found that ICT self-efficacy and organizational support for blended learning predicted attitudes toward blended learning, which in turn predicted its use, jointly explaining 54.7 percent of variance in usage. Their study is adopted as the base paper (see Section 2.2) because it is the only workplace-adjacent blended-learning study identified in this review that both tests a formal mediation model and publishes its own full measurement instrument, a requirement for the base paper specifically. Its own mediator, attitude toward blended learning, and its own outcome, use of blended learning, differ from the mediator and outcome examined in the present study; the present study's departure from this base paper is accordingly substantial rather than incremental, discussed further in Section 2.2.

The closest match to the present study's actual mechanism, though not itself the base paper because its measurement items are not published, is Saroj, Sahney and Sekar (2026), who examined workplace blended-learning training among 375 employees and modelled learner engagement as a mediator between blended-learning acceptance, instructor competence, and metacognitive skills on one hand, and employee agility on the other, with supervisor support tested as a moderator of the engagement–agility relationship. Their structural equation modelling analysis, conducted using SPSS and AMOS and independently verified against the publisher's own record for this review, found that acceptance, instructor competence, and metacognitive skills each positively predicted engagement, that engagement in turn predicted employee agility, and that supervisor support strengthened this latter relationship. The present study's own engagement-mediation logic is modelled directly on this study rather than on the base paper: Where Saroj, Sahney and Sekar (2026) trace engagement through to employee agility, the present study instead traces engagement through training effectiveness to knowledge retention and, finally, job performance, a longer Kirkpatrick-aligned chain motivated by this study's specific interest in training outcomes rather than the broader construct of workplace agility. The present study also retains trainer competence as a second antecedent of engagement alongside hybrid learning itself, following this study's finding that instructor competence predicts engagement; this is the strongest available support for testing trainer competence at all, since it is not tested in the base paper itself, a limitation acknowledged directly in Section 2.2. Metacognitive skill, an individual-difference variable tested by Saroj, Sahney and Sekar (2026) as a third antecedent, is not included here; its omission is a scope decision discussed further in Section 1.3, not an assessment that it is unimportant.

Yang (2023) similarly modelled a chain from organizational support through ICT self-efficacy and engagement to satisfaction among 245 participants, again supporting engagement's centrality as a psychological mechanism in blended-learning outcomes. Hassan, Bashir and Arshad (2020), examining a composite learning-engagement construct among 498 business-school respondents, provide one of the few engagement studies situated in a business rather than a general-education setting, lending some support to the applicability of engagement-centred models like the one developed here beyond purely academic contexts.

### Knowledge retention in blended training

Retention of training content beyond the immediate post-training period is comparatively under-measured in the blended-learning literature, which tends to privilege immediate learning outcomes over longer-term recall. Wakekar et al. (2026), in a comparative study of 248 community health workers assigned to blended, traditional, and e-learning-only training, measured knowledge retention at six months and found substantially higher retention in the blended group (78.2 percent) than in the traditional (62.4 percent) or e-learning-only (58.7 percent) groups. This finding provides empirical support for treating retention as a distinct downstream outcome of blended training rather than folding it into a single effectiveness measure, though it is notable that Wakekar et al. operationalized retention as an objective recall-test score rather than a multi-item self-report perception scale of the kind adopted in this study; this distinction is addressed further in Chapter 3.

### Training transfer and job performance

The final stage of the outcome chain examined in this study — the translation of training into workplace performance — draws on a longer-established literature on training transfer. Baldwin and Ford's (1988) foundational review identified trainee characteristics, training design, and the work environment as the three principal determinants of transfer, a framework subsequently updated by Blume, Ford, Baldwin and Huang (2010) through meta-analysis, which confirmed the continuing importance of work-environment and climate factors as moderators of transfer. Facteau, Dobbins, Russell, Ladd and Kudisch (1995) extended this line of enquiry by demonstrating that organizational commitment and multiple sources of social support predict pre-training motivation, which in turn predicts perceived transfer, establishing an early empirical link between organizational support and training outcomes via a motivational mechanism. For the measurement of job performance itself, this study draws on the task-performance and contextual-performance distinction established by Williams and Anderson (1991) and later operationalized in the Individual Work Performance Questionnaire (Koopmans et al., 2011, 2014), an instrument validated across occupational sectors and multiple national contexts. On the measurement side of transfer specifically, Holton, Bates and Ruona (2000) developed the Learning Transfer System Inventory, a sixteen-factor instrument covering motivational, environmental, and content-validity determinants of transfer, later cross-culturally replicated by Yamnill and McLean (2005) in a non-Western, Thai organizational sample with a factor structure closely matching the original. This cross-cultural replication is a useful precedent for the present study's own use of instruments developed largely in Western settings within an Indian organizational sample; it does not remove the need for contextual adaptation but does suggest that the underlying constructs travel reasonably well across national settings.

### Organizational and supervisor support: a construct considered but not modelled

Organizational and supervisor support recurs across the literature reviewed above as a plausible contributor to training outcomes, and is addressed here for completeness even though it is not included as a construct in the model tested in this study. Eisenberger, Huntington, Hutchison and Sowa (1986) originated the concept of perceived organizational support, the general theoretical basis from which most later, training-specific support measures are derived. Facteau, Dobbins, Russell, Ladd and Kudisch (1995), discussed above in relation to training transfer, found that organizational commitment and multiple sources of social support predicted pre-training motivation, which in turn predicted perceived transfer, one of the earlier empirical demonstrations that support operates through a motivational pathway rather than directly on outcomes. Ye, Kuang and Liu (2022), whose engagement findings are discussed above, operationalized organizational support for blended learning specifically as a four-item scale and found it a significant predictor of attitudes toward blended learning. Saroj, Sahney and Sekar (2026), the closest match to this study's own mechanism though not itself the base paper (see Section 2.2), tested supervisor support not as an antecedent of engagement but as a moderator strengthening the relationship between engagement and employee agility, a role distinct from the antecedent role tested in the studies above. Despite this body of evidence, organizational and supervisor support is not included as a formal construct in the present study's model, a scope decision made primarily on statistical grounds given the sample size available and discussed further in Section 1.3 and in Chapter 5's directions for future research; its exclusion here reflects a deliberate boundary on the model tested, not an assessment that the construct is unimportant.

### Training effectiveness in the Indian banking sector

Research specifically addressing training effectiveness within Indian banking remains limited. Jain and Jain (2015) examined training effectiveness across public, private, and foreign banks operating in India using a five-dimension construct — commitment to training, access, relevance, benefits, and satisfaction — and found no significant variation in training effectiveness by bank ownership type. A more recent study applied Kirkpatrick's four-level evaluation model to a sample of 402 managerial and non-managerial employees across Indian banks, reporting that the four levels were interlinked and that the model was effective for evaluating training's impact on motivation and bank performance; this study is one of the few to apply Kirkpatrick's framework within the Indian banking context specifically, though its findings speak to the model's descriptive applicability rather than to the explanatory mechanisms examined in the present research.

### Summary of the review

Read together, this literature establishes three things with reasonable confidence and leaves a fourth largely open. It confirms that blended and hybrid learning, when thoughtfully integrated rather than merely combined, is generally associated with positive learning and training outcomes across both higher-education and, more thinly, workplace settings. It confirms that learner engagement is a recurring and theoretically well-grounded mechanism through which this association operates, supported both by general engagement theory and by workplace-specific evidence, most directly Saroj, Sahney and Sekar (2026). It confirms that training's effects extend beyond the immediate learning event into retention and, further downstream, into job performance, though these later stages are measured far less consistently than immediate learning outcomes. What the literature does not yet establish is how these pieces fit together within a single organizational setting, and specifically within Indian public-sector banking: engagement, retention, and transfer are each studied, but rarely within the same model, and almost never within this sector. This gap, developed formally in Section 2.4, is what the present study's conceptual model, presented next, is designed to address.

## 2.2 Theoretical Framework

This study draws on six bodies of theory to construct its conceptual model: training-evaluation theory, engagement theory, technology-acceptance theory, self-determination theory, blended-learning design theory, and training-transfer theory. Each is discussed in turn below, together with the specific part of the model it justifies, before the six are synthesized into the model tested in Chapter 3.

### Training-evaluation theory: Kirkpatrick's four levels and Holton's critique

Kirkpatrick's (1994) four-level model, reaction, learning, behaviour, and results, provides the overarching logic for the outcome chain examined in this study. In Kirkpatrick's original formulation, reaction measures how trainees respond to the training experience itself; learning measures the knowledge or skill actually acquired; behaviour measures whether that learning is applied on the job; and results measure the resulting organizational or business outcome. This study's own outcome chain follows the same successive logic without claiming a one-to-one mapping: Training Effectiveness corresponds most closely to Kirkpatrick's learning level, Knowledge Retention extends this into the behaviour level by asking whether learning persists and is available for use beyond the training event itself, and Job Performance corresponds to the results level. Treating these as successive rather than interchangeable outcomes, as Kirkpatrick's model requires, is the theoretical basis for measuring all three separately in this study rather than collapsing them into a single "training effectiveness" score.

Holton (1996) offered an influential critique of Kirkpatrick's model, arguing that it describes outcome levels without explaining the conditions under which one level actually leads to the next, and proposing instead that ability, motivation, and environmental factors jointly determine whether learning translates into individual and organizational performance. This critique provides the theoretical justification for including learner engagement, and, in this study's model, trainer competence, as explanatory mechanisms positioned between hybrid learning and its outcomes, rather than treating training delivery mode as directly predictive of training effectiveness, retention, or performance on its own.

### Engagement theory: behavioural, emotional, and cognitive components

Fredricks, Blumenfeld and Paris (2004) established a three-component structure of engagement, behavioural engagement, understood as effort, persistence, and participation; emotional engagement, understood as interest, enjoyment, and a sense of belonging; and cognitive engagement, understood as the use of strategic, self-regulated learning approaches, that underpins most subsequent engagement scales, including the workplace-training-specific measures discussed in Section 2.1. This three-component structure matters theoretically for the present study because it positions engagement as more than a single attitude toward training: an employee can be behaviourally present without being cognitively engaged, or emotionally positive about a session without applying self-regulated learning strategies to it, and each of these components plausibly transmits the effects of hybrid learning design differently. It is this multi-component richness that supports treating engagement as a genuine explanatory mechanism, capable of carrying the effect of hybrid learning design through to training effectiveness, rather than as a simple proxy for satisfaction. The validated instruments discussed in Section 2.1, the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (Schaufeli, Bakker & Salanova, 2006) and the Safety Training Engagement Scale, can both be read as operationalizations of this same underlying three-component structure, measuring vigour or dedication as behavioural and emotional indicators and absorption as a cognitive one, which is why this study is able to draw on established engagement measurement traditions rather than treating engagement as a construct requiring measurement from first principles.

### Technology-acceptance theory and computer self-efficacy

Davis's (1989) Technology Acceptance Model proposes that an individual's intention to use a technology, and their actual use of it, is determined jointly by perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. Compeau and Higgins (1995) extended this line of theory with the construct of computer self-efficacy, an individual's belief in their own capability to use computing technology, which predicts technology-related outcomes such as anxiety and ease-of-use perceptions independently of actual skill level. Together, these two frameworks provide the theoretical basis for the technology-readiness items incorporated into this study's hybrid-learning construct: an employee's engagement with the digital component of a hybrid training programme, such as Union Bank of India's Union Vidya application described in Section 1.5, plausibly depends not only on the platform's objective usability but on the employee's own perceived ease of use and confidence in using it, both of which this theoretical pairing predicts directly.

### Self-determination theory: a psychological account of why flexibility matters

Ryan and Deci's (2000) self-determination theory identifies autonomy, competence, and relatedness as three basic psychological needs whose satisfaction supports intrinsic motivation. Self-paced, learner-controlled formats, of the kind hybrid learning typically offers through its digital component, plausibly support the autonomy need in particular, offering a psychological account of why flexible training formats might be experienced more positively than rigid, fully classroom-based ones. Motivation itself is not modelled as a separate construct in the present study, a scope decision discussed further in Section 1.3; self-determination theory is nonetheless retained here because it offers a plausible underlying account of why hybrid learning's flexibility might be expected to increase learner engagement specifically, the mechanism this study does model, rather than acting on training outcomes directly.

### Blended-learning design theory: thoughtful integration

Garrison and Kanuka's (2004) definition of blended learning as the thoughtful integration, rather than the mere co-occurrence, of online and face-to-face modes is not only a definitional starting point, discussed in Section 2.1, but a theoretical claim in its own right: that integration quality, and not simply the presence of both delivery modes, is what should predict training effectiveness. This claim directly justifies treating Integration as one of the measured dimensions of the hybrid-learning construct in this study's instrument, rather than measuring only the presence or proportion of digital content, since Garrison and Kanuka's theory predicts that two programmes with an identical proportion of digital content could differ substantially in effectiveness depending on how deliberately the two modes are sequenced to reinforce one another. Boelens, De Wever and Voet (2017), discussed in Section 2.1, translate this integration principle into four concrete design challenges, flexibility, interaction, facilitation of the learning process, and the affective learning climate, each of which a well-integrated hybrid programme must resolve; poorly resolved, any one of these four can undermine engagement and effectiveness regardless of how much digital content a programme contains, reinforcing the theoretical point that integration quality, not digital proportion, is what should predict outcomes.

### Training-transfer theory: from retention to performance

The final link in this study's outcome chain, from Knowledge Retention to Job Performance, is theoretically grounded in training-transfer research rather than in training-evaluation or engagement theory directly. Baldwin and Ford's (1988) foundational review identified three determinants of whether learning transfers into workplace performance: trainee characteristics, training design, and the work environment, establishing that retained knowledge does not automatically become applied performance without favourable conditions across all three. Blume, Ford, Baldwin and Huang (2010), updating this framework through meta-analysis, confirmed that work-environment and climate factors continue to moderate transfer decades after Baldwin and Ford's original review, meaning the retention-to-performance link this study tests cannot be assumed to be automatic or uniform across contexts. This theoretical caution is directly relevant to the present study's own model: Knowledge Retention is hypothesized to predict Job Performance, but training-transfer theory implies that this relationship may be weaker, or more context-dependent, than the earlier links in the chain, a possibility this study's mediation analysis is specifically designed to detect rather than assume away.

### Synthesizing the framework: Model-Alpha

Taken together, these five bodies of theory support a model in which Hybrid Learning and Trainer Competence act as parallel antecedents of Learner Engagement, following the mechanism logic established by Holton's critique of Kirkpatrick and given empirical precedent by Saroj, Sahney and Sekar (2026), discussed in Section 2.1 — the closest match to this study's own mechanism, though the study's base paper for measurement purposes is Ye, Kuang and Liu (2022), since Saroj, Sahney and Sekar (2026) does not publish its own instrument; Learner Engagement, understood through Fredricks, Blumenfeld and Paris's three-component structure, mediates the relationship between these antecedents and Training Effectiveness; and Training Effectiveness in turn predicts Knowledge Retention and, ultimately, Job Performance, following Kirkpatrick's successive-levels logic. Technology-acceptance theory and self-determination theory do not appear as separate constructs in this chain, but inform, respectively, the technology-readiness dimension of the Hybrid Learning construct and the theoretical account of why hybrid learning's flexibility is expected to shape engagement specifically. Blended-learning design theory, in turn, justifies measuring integration quality as a dimension of Hybrid Learning rather than treating the format as a simple binary of digital versus classroom content. Training-transfer theory, finally, justifies treating the Knowledge Retention to Job Performance link as a relationship to be tested rather than assumed, since Baldwin and Ford's determinants imply this final link is the one most likely to be conditional on factors outside this study's model. This six-construct model, Hybrid Learning and Trainer Competence, Learner Engagement, Training Effectiveness, Knowledge Retention, and Job Performance, is referred to throughout this dissertation as Model-Alpha.

## 2.4 Gaps in the Study

Four gaps in the existing literature inform the present research design. Each is stated below together with the specific way this study's design responds to it, rather than as an abstract observation about the field.

First, an explanatory gap: the literature reviewed above establishes that blended learning is generally associated with positive outcomes, but comparatively few studies examine the psychological or organizational mechanisms through which this association arises. Ashraf et al.'s (2021) umbrella review of fifty-seven systematic reviews found engagement, satisfaction, and self-regulation studied primarily as outcomes rather than as tested explanatory mechanisms within a formal model. It is worth noting that this is not a complete absence of mechanism research: Ye, Kuang and Liu (2022) and Yang (2023), discussed in Section 2.1, do model a mechanism, but a technology-adoption one, running from ICT self-efficacy and organizational support through attitudes to use. The gap this study addresses is narrower and more specific: engagement as a psychological mechanism, distinct from technology adoption, remains comparatively untested as a mediator between training design and training effectiveness. This study addresses that gap directly by modelling Learner Engagement as a formal mediator, tested through the Baron and Kenny procedure and a Sobel test rather than assumed from a simple correlation, corresponding to hypotheses H5a-c in Chapter 3.

Second, a population gap: the same literature is concentrated overwhelmingly in higher-education and student samples. Bin Mubayrik's (2018) review of workplace blended learning identified only seventeen qualifying studies against a substantially larger higher-education literature, indicating that findings from student populations cannot be assumed to generalize to organizational training contexts. This is not only a sampling concern: the psychological conditions under which a student engages with blended coursework, largely voluntary, with grades as the primary incentive, differ materially from those facing an employee attending mandatory job-related training, where incentives include career progression, job security, and immediate applicability to daily work. A mechanism validated in a student population cannot be assumed to operate identically in an employee population for this reason, not only because the samples differ demographically. This study addresses the gap directly by sampling exclusively from a working employee population, approximately eighty respondents at Union Bank of India, rather than students or a mixed sample.

Third, a geographic and sectoral gap: very few studies address blended or hybrid learning specifically within Indian public-sector banking. Jain and Jain (2015) examined training effectiveness generally across Indian banks but did not examine hybrid learning as a construct at all, finding no significant difference in their five-dimension training-effectiveness measure by bank ownership type; a finding about training effectiveness in general terms, not evidence about hybrid or blended delivery specifically. The more recent Kirkpatrick-based Indian-banking study discussed in Section 2.1 applies an established evaluation framework within Indian banking but, again, does not examine hybrid learning as its subject. No verified study was located, despite a direct search, that examines hybrid learning specifically within Indian public-sector banking, still less within Union Bank of India itself, as discussed further in Section 1.4. This study addresses the gap directly by being conducted within exactly this setting.

Fourth, a chain-depth gap: no study identified in this review tests a full sequential chain from hybrid learning through engagement, training effectiveness, knowledge retention, and job performance within a single model. Saroj, Sahney and Sekar (2026), the closest match to this study's own mechanism, test engagement as a mediator across three antecedents but conclude at employee agility, a single downstream outcome, rather than extending further into training effectiveness, retention, or performance; Wakekar et al. (2026) measure retention directly and rigorously, at a six-month interval, but do not model engagement or a subsequent performance stage at all; Lee (2010) examines transfer into the workplace but without a formal mediation model or an engagement construct. Each of these three studies tests one or two links of the chain this study proposes; none tests all five. This study addresses the gap directly by testing the full sequence, Hybrid Learning and Trainer Competence through Learner Engagement, Training Effectiveness, Knowledge Retention, and Job Performance, as the individual path hypotheses H1, H1b through H4 in Chapter 3, together with the serial-mediation hypotheses H5a-c, which test whether each intermediate step in the chain genuinely mediates rather than merely correlates with the next.

Taken together, these four gaps support the following statement of the research problem: while substantial literature confirms that blended and hybrid learning are associated with improved engagement, satisfaction, and learning outcomes, this evidence is concentrated in higher education and does not adequately explain the psychological mechanisms through which hybrid learning contributes to workplace training effectiveness, nor has it been examined within the specific context of Indian public-sector banking, nor has any study yet tested a complete outcome chain extending from engagement through to job performance. Organizational and supervisor support, discussed in Section 2.1 as a construct considered but not modelled, is a related but distinct scope decision rather than a fifth gap: the literature on support is comparatively well developed, and its exclusion here reflects this study's own sample-size constraints rather than an absence of prior research to build on. The present study addresses the four gaps above concurrently by examining a hybrid-learning model, mediated by learner engagement, among employees of Union Bank of India.
