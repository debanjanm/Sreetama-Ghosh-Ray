# Chapter 2 — Review of Literature

## 2.1 Overview of Relevant Review

### Hybrid learning as a workplace training variable

Hybrid learning, often called blended learning, combines digital learning and face-to-face learning within one planned programme. The term is sometimes used loosely to describe any training that contains both a classroom session and an online resource. That description is incomplete. A hybrid programme requires a connection between the two modes. The online element should prepare, support, extend, or reinforce the face-to-face element, while the face-to-face element should clarify, practise, discuss, or apply the learning introduced digitally.

Garrison and Kanuka (2004) describe blended learning as the thoughtful integration of online and face-to-face learning. Their work was written in a higher-education context, but the main idea applies to workplace training. A bank employee may complete a digital module on a new procedure before attending a trainer-led session. The classroom session can then be used for questions, demonstrations, practice cases, and discussion of exceptions. A later online quiz, job aid, or refresher module can help the employee revisit the procedure after returning to work. The programme is hybrid when these stages form a sequence rather than when the same content is merely repeated in two formats.

Graham (2006) also presents blended learning as a deliberate combination of face-to-face instruction and computer-mediated instruction. He explains that the balance between the two modes depends on the purpose of the learning activity. Information that employees need to read, revisit, or complete at their own pace may suit a digital format. Activities requiring immediate clarification, demonstration, role play, or group problem-solving may need a trainer-led setting. This distinction is useful in banking. Product information, compliance updates, cybersecurity reminders, and system instructions can be distributed digitally, whereas customer situations, problem diagnosis, and supervised practice may benefit from in-person interaction.

The present study uses Hybrid Learning as the independent variable. It does not compare digital-only training with classroom-only training. Instead, it asks employees how they experienced the quality of the combined programme. The variable therefore concerns whether the modes were connected, whether the digital platform was usable, whether content was accessible and flexible, and whether the programme supported interaction and feedback.

### Integration of digital and face-to-face learning

The integration of modes is the first important dimension of Hybrid Learning. When employees cannot see a connection between the online and classroom components, the programme may feel fragmented. A digital module may then become an administrative requirement rather than preparation for learning. Equally, a classroom session may repeat online material instead of extending it.

Han and Ellis (2020) developed the Perceptions of the Blended Learning Environment Questionnaire. Their scale was validated with university students, so it cannot be treated as a corporate employee scale without adaptation. It remains valuable because it identifies three aspects that can be examined in a blended environment: the integration of online and face-to-face learning, the contribution of the online component, and the workload associated with online learning. For the present study, the first two aspects are particularly relevant. Employees need to know how the digital material relates to later sessions and whether it helps them understand or apply the subject.

Boelens, De Wever and Voet (2017) reviewed blended-learning design and identified flexibility, interaction, facilitation of the learning process, and the affective learning climate as recurring design challenges. Their review is again largely grounded in education, but the challenges are relevant to employee training. Hybrid learning does not become effective merely because it offers convenience. Employees may need clear sequencing, meaningful activities, access to assistance, and a setting in which they can ask questions without embarrassment. These considerations guide the Hybrid Learning items in the present questionnaire.

Kumar and Pande (2017) bring the discussion closer to working professionals. Their study of a technology-mediated learning ecosystem identifies institutional, faculty-related, learner-related, and pedagogy-related conditions that affect blended learning for professionals. They emphasize that working learners have different needs from full-time students. Their learning is shaped by work schedules, prior experience, access to technology, the usefulness of learning material, and the immediate relevance of the subject. The article does not provide a ready-made validated questionnaire. It does, however, identify practical aspects of hybrid delivery that can be converted carefully into questionnaire dimensions: access to technical resources, suitable learning media, feedback, time management, and the connection between online and in-person activities.

### LMS access, usability, and digital learning content

The digital platform is part of the employee’s hybrid-learning experience. If an LMS is difficult to access, slow, confusing, or missing relevant material, the employee may not receive the intended benefit of the online component. Conversely, a platform that provides clear modules, accessible materials, reminders, and opportunities to revisit content can support later preparation and revision.

Mulaudzi (2021) examined blended teaching in workplace training in the South African transport sector. The study used a structured questionnaire with employees and training personnel. Although it is a Master’s dissertation rather than a peer-reviewed journal article, it is useful because it considers an actual workplace rather than a student course. The study highlights respondents’ views on time management, connectivity, experience with the learning system, and satisfaction with blended delivery. These are not adopted as a validated scale, but they support the inclusion of access, reliability, and flexibility in the present Hybrid Learning variable.

The LMS must also be considered in relation to the organization being studied. Union Bank of India’s 2024–25 annual report describes Union Vidya as the Bank’s new Learning Management System. The report states that the platform supports structured learning pathways and provides access to mandatory and specialized learning. It also describes a broader Learning and Development approach that includes e-learning, virtual sessions, and staggered programmes. This confirms that a digital learning platform is relevant to the organization’s training context. It does not establish that every employee in the selected regional office used Union Vidya in the same way or attended the same programme. For this reason, the questionnaire asks about the digital platform used for the respondent’s most recent hybrid-training programme and gives Union Vidya only as an example where applicable.

LMS questions in this study are therefore limited to an employee’s experience of access, navigation, material availability, and technical reliability. The study does not use LMS log-in records, completion records, or internal system data. It also does not evaluate Union Vidya as a product. The focus is the employee’s perception of whether the digital component supported the connected training programme.

### Flexibility, interaction, and trainer guidance

Flexibility is often identified as a potential benefit of hybrid learning. An employee may be able to complete an online module around work demands, revisit a recording, or review a document before a workshop. However, flexibility does not mean that all online learning is automatically convenient. It depends on workload, time allowed by the organization, access to a device or network, and the design of the module. An employee who can technically log in but has no practical time to complete a module may not experience flexibility.

Kumar and Pande (2017) note that a blended-learning system for working professionals must respond to learner needs and the work context. Mulaudzi (2021) similarly identifies time management and reliable connectivity as important in workplace blended learning. These findings support two questions in the present instrument: whether the digital component could be completed at a suitable time and whether employees could revisit modules or material for revision. The questions do not measure personal digital literacy as an independent variable. They measure whether the design of the programme gave the employee practical flexibility.

Interaction and trainer guidance are also part of hybrid delivery. Employees may need to ask questions after completing a digital module, obtain feedback on an activity, or understand how an online task connects with an upcoming workshop. Boelens et al. (2017) treat facilitation and interaction as important challenges in blended-learning design. Kumar and Pande (2017) also point to the role of facilitators and feedback. In the current study, questions about explanation, opportunity to ask questions, participation, and feedback are retained within Hybrid Learning. The study does not test trainer competence or learner engagement as separate variables. This decision keeps the model aligned with the guide’s instruction to use Hybrid Learning as the broad independent variable.

### Training effectiveness as a workplace outcome

Training effectiveness is broader than trainee satisfaction. A respondent may enjoy a session but not understand the content, remember it, or use it at work. A programme may also demand effort from an employee and still be useful if it improves knowledge, skills, and work performance. The present study therefore defines Training Effectiveness as the employee’s perception that a recent training programme produced useful learning and supported its application to relevant work.

Kirkpatrick’s (1994) four-level framework is widely used in training evaluation. The four levels are reaction, learning, behaviour, and results. Reaction concerns how participants respond to the programme. Learning concerns the knowledge, skills, or attitudes acquired. Behaviour concerns whether learning is used in the work setting. Results concern wider organizational outcomes. The framework is useful because it shows that an assessment of training should not stop at whether employees were satisfied with the programme.

At the same time, Kirkpatrick’s framework is not a complete explanation of training outcomes. Holton (1996) argues that the four levels describe possible outcomes but do not fully explain why learning transfers to work. Factors such as trainee characteristics, training design, and the work environment affect the process. The present study does not attempt to measure every influencing factor. It uses a focused employee-perception measure of training effectiveness that covers learning, relevance, transfer, and perceived individual work improvement.

Aziz (2015) developed and validated the General Training Effectiveness Scale for workplace learning. The final scale contains ten items grouped under learning performance, individual performance, and organizational performance. The study is useful because it was designed for employee training rather than academic coursework. Its learning-performance items concern understanding and the ability to use what was taught. Its individual-performance items concern whether training contributes to professional performance and job improvement. The organizational-performance items extend to department productivity and organizational reputation.

The present study uses Aziz’s logic selectively. Employees can reasonably report whether they understood a programme, found it relevant, used the learning, and perceived improvement in their own work. They are less able to judge whether one programme improved the Bank’s overall productivity or reputation. For that reason, the questionnaire retains learning and individual work improvement but excludes organization-wide performance claims. The questionnaire also uses fresh, banking-context wording rather than copying the original item text.

### Learning, work relevance, transfer, and individual work improvement

Training effectiveness begins with learning. Employees need to understand the main subject matter and feel able to use the knowledge or skills taught. In a banking context, the learning may concern systems, products, compliance procedures, service standards, digital tools, or another work-related topic. The present instrument asks whether employees understood the main knowledge or skills, whether they learned information useful in their current role, and whether they feel capable of using the skill taught.

Work relevance is closely related to learning. Content that does not connect with an employee’s responsibilities may be difficult to retain or apply. Baldwin and Ford (1988) identify trainee characteristics, training design, and the work environment as major influences on the transfer of training. Their model explains that learning acquired during a programme must be generalized to the job and maintained over time. Relevance is important because employees are more likely to apply learning when they can recognize the work situation to which it applies.

Training transfer refers to the application of learning in the work environment. It is a central part of training effectiveness because the purpose of organizational training is normally to support a work-related change. Blume, Ford, Baldwin and Huang (2010), in their meta-analytic review, confirm that transfer is influenced by factors beyond the training room, including the work environment and the opportunity to use learning. A cross-sectional employee survey cannot verify an objective performance change. It can, however, ask employees whether they have applied the learning, feel confident using it, and received enough practice or examples to do so.

Individual work improvement is the final aspect covered by the present dependent variable. Employees are asked whether applying learning has helped them perform relevant tasks accurately, efficiently, and with better quality, and whether training has increased their confidence in related responsibilities. These are self-reported outcomes. They should be interpreted as employees’ perceptions, not as a substitute for supervisor ratings, audit records, service indicators, or productivity data.

### Evidence connecting hybrid learning and training effectiveness

The relationship examined in this dissertation is direct: whether a more positive Hybrid Learning experience is associated with higher perceived Training Effectiveness. The literature provides reasons to expect such a relationship. A connected digital and face-to-face programme can give employees a flexible route to foundational content, provide a forum for questions and practice, and make digital resources available for later revision. These features may help employees understand the material and use it in work situations.

Uddin, Ahamed, Jakowan, Islam and Nahar (2026) provide the closest recent workplace study for the broad topic. Their study examines employees with blended-training experience and tests the relationship between blended learning, soft-skill development, knowledge acquisition, and employee performance. The article supports the proposition that blended learning can be linked with work-related employee outcomes. Its model is not reproduced in the present study. The current dissertation does not test soft-skill development, knowledge acquisition, and employee performance as separate mediators or outcomes. Instead, it focuses on a more direct and manageable relationship between employees’ hybrid-learning experience and their view of training effectiveness.

Kumar and Pande (2017) and Mulaudzi (2021) add practical support for this relationship. Both sources indicate that technology access, appropriate design, reliable connectivity, suitable learning arrangements, and learner support influence how working people experience blended learning. Aziz (2015), Baldwin and Ford (1988), and Blume et al. (2010) show that effective employee training should be evaluated through learning and work application. Taken together, these studies justify examining whether employees who experience a well-integrated and accessible hybrid programme also report stronger learning and work-related outcomes.

### Separate review streams and their relevance to the present study

The review has deliberately considered Hybrid Learning and Training Effectiveness as separate bodies of literature before bringing them together. This is necessary because the two terms answer different questions. Hybrid-learning literature asks what an integrated digital and face-to-face environment should provide. It considers the relationship between learning modes, access to digital tools, sequencing, flexibility, facilitation, and interaction. Training-effectiveness literature asks what useful training achieves. It considers learning, work relevance, transfer, and changes in individual work practice.

Treating the variables separately also prevents a common problem in questionnaire design. If a statement such as “the online platform was easy to use” is placed in a Training Effectiveness scale, the scale can confuse the quality of delivery with the outcome of training. Similarly, a statement such as “I perform my work more accurately” should not be placed in the Hybrid Learning scale because it concerns an outcome after learning. The present instrument keeps delivery-experience items under Hybrid Learning and outcome items under Training Effectiveness.

The separate review also shows why the questionnaire is context-adapted. Hybrid-learning studies frequently use student samples and therefore use terms such as course, lecturer, classmates, and assessment. Workplace training requires a different vocabulary. Employees attend programmes while managing work responsibilities, and they may use internal digital platforms, mandatory modules, policy updates, or job-related demonstrations. The Version 2 questionnaire uses the terms training programme, trainer, digital platform, learning material, and work task. These terms are intended to be clear to employees across job levels without claiming that the questionnaire is a previously validated bank-specific scale.

Likewise, Training Effectiveness cannot be measured solely through whether employees enjoyed a programme. Immediate satisfaction can be useful feedback for a training department, but it is not enough to establish whether employees understood the content or could apply it. The present outcome questions therefore move from understanding and relevance to use and perceived individual work improvement. This order reflects the training-evaluation and transfer literature while keeping the dependent variable within the limits of a short self-report survey.

The two streams meet in the central proposition of this study. A hybrid programme is expected to be more effective when it gives employees a coherent path through digital and face-to-face learning and reduces avoidable barriers to participation. The study does not claim that every digital feature produces better training. The relationship must be examined from the responses of the selected employees. A positive result would show an association in this group; it would not prove that the Hybrid Learning experience alone caused later work improvement.

## 2.2 Theoretical Framework

The study uses three complementary perspectives: blended-learning integration, training evaluation, and training transfer. They are used together to explain the relationship between Hybrid Learning and Training Effectiveness.

### Blended-learning integration

The first perspective is the integration principle proposed in blended-learning literature. Garrison and Kanuka (2004) and Graham (2006) explain that the value of blended learning lies in the purposeful combination of modes. Digital learning can support preparation, independent review, and access to material. Face-to-face learning can support discussion, feedback, demonstration, and practice. The theory does not imply that one fixed combination is best for every topic. It suggests that the design should match the purpose of the learning activity.

In this study, a positive Hybrid Learning experience is indicated when employees perceive that the two modes are connected, the digital platform can be used without unnecessary barriers, content is available for revision, and interaction is supported. These features are expected to help employees engage with the programme as a coherent learning experience.

### Training evaluation

The second perspective is Kirkpatrick’s training-evaluation framework. The framework distinguishes reaction, learning, behaviour, and results. It guides the present study away from a satisfaction-only measure. Questions in the Training Effectiveness section ask about understanding, relevance, use of learning, and perceived work improvement. They cover the learning and individual-behaviour logic of the framework while remaining appropriate for a short self-report survey.

Holton’s (1996) critique is also relevant. Training evaluation should consider the factors that enable later work application rather than assume that a positive reaction becomes effective performance. The current study does not measure every factor identified by Holton. Its Hybrid Learning items nevertheless examine features of the training design that employees directly experience, including access, sequencing, guidance, feedback, and opportunity for participation.

### Training transfer

The third perspective is Baldwin and Ford’s (1988) training-transfer model. The model explains that learning becomes useful when it is generalized to work situations and maintained over time. Training transfer depends on the learner, the design of the training, and the work environment. In this study, transfer is included within Training Effectiveness because the employee is asked whether the programme helped with real work tasks and whether the employee has applied the knowledge or skills.

The model does not claim that Hybrid Learning alone determines transfer. Workload, manager support, opportunity to use learning, and the nature of the job may also influence whether an employee applies a new skill. The study acknowledges these factors as limitations. It tests a focused relationship: whether the perceived quality of Hybrid Learning is associated with the employee’s perception of training effectiveness.

### Conceptual framework

The conceptual framework is simple and consistent with the research objective:

```text
Hybrid Learning                                      Training Effectiveness

• integration of learning modes                      • learning and understanding
• LMS access and usability                            • relevance to work
• flexibility and digital content          ─────▶     • application and transfer
• interaction and trainer guidance                    • individual work improvement
```

The dimensions on the left are measured as one Hybrid Learning score. The dimensions on the right are measured as one Training Effectiveness score. The model has one direct relationship and does not propose that each dimension is an independent statistical variable.

## 2.3 Case Study: Union Bank of India

The study is situated at Union Bank of India, a public-sector bank, with planned data collection among employees connected with Regional Office Chennai South in Royapettah, Chennai. Banking is a suitable setting for a study of training effectiveness because employees need current knowledge of products, customer service, regulations, risk controls, digital systems, and internal procedures. Training often has to reach employees in different locations while maintaining common standards.

Union Bank of India’s 2024–25 annual report describes an active Learning and Development system. The report states that the Bank has introduced Union Vidya as its Learning Management System. According to the report, the platform supports mandatory and specialized learning, structured learning pathways, e-learning, virtual sessions, and staggered programmes. These features make the organization relevant to a study of Hybrid Learning. They indicate that employees may experience a combination of digital resources and trainer-led or face-to-face training.

The case study does not assume that all staff have identical access, training requirements, or platform experience. Nor does it claim that every programme has the same balance of online and face-to-face activities. The empirical survey asks eligible employees about one recent programme that combined both modes. This approach keeps the study focused on actual employee experience while avoiding unsupported claims about the entire Bank’s training system.

The selected setting also offers a useful research context because the published literature does not provide a verified study of Hybrid Learning and Training Effectiveness specifically among Union Bank employees. A public-sector banking organization has different work demands from a university classroom or a general corporate sample. Employees may need to learn within a schedule shaped by customer service, operational targets, compliance requirements, and the use of internal systems. The findings of the present study are therefore intended to provide a focused account of the selected employee group rather than a conclusion about every bank employee in India.

## 2.4 Gaps in the Study

The review identifies four related gaps.

The first is a **workplace evidence gap**. Much of the blended-learning literature and many validated blended-environment instruments are based on students in higher education. Han and Ellis (2020) and Boelens et al. (2017) provide useful concepts, but they do not examine employee training. Kumar and Pande (2017), Mulaudzi (2021), and Uddin et al. (2026) provide workplace evidence, yet the number of directly relevant corporate studies remains much smaller than the education literature. The present study responds by examining employees who have attended hybrid training.

The second is a **banking-sector gap**. Training-effectiveness research is well established, and organizations in many sectors use digital learning. However, the review did not identify a verified study that directly tests Hybrid Learning and Training Effectiveness among employees of Union Bank of India. The local setting is therefore relevant because the Bank has a documented LMS and an established learning system, while employee perceptions of the connected training experience remain underexamined in the reviewed literature.

The third is an **LMS-context gap**. Studies often discuss online access or technology in general terms. The employee’s actual experience of an LMS can determine whether digital content is practical to use before, during, and after face-to-face training. The present study includes access, navigation, material availability, and technical reliability as Hybrid Learning dimensions. It does not treat the LMS as a separate variable or make a product-performance claim about Union Vidya.

The fourth is a **measurement and model gap**. Existing literature offers a blended-learning environment scale largely validated with students and a workplace Training Effectiveness Scale that includes organization-level outcomes. Neither can be transferred unchanged to a short bank-employee questionnaire. The present study develops a 26-item, literature-informed instrument that retains the relevant content: integration, LMS experience, flexibility, interaction, learning, work relevance, application, and individual work improvement. It uses two broad scores so the model remains suitable for the proposed sample and the guide’s instruction.

A final **implementation gap** concerns the relationship between a formal LMS and actual training use. An organization may provide a digital platform and a range of learning resources, yet employees may still experience different levels of access, clarity, guidance, or integration across programmes. The Union Bank annual report establishes the availability of Union Vidya and the Bank’s wider learning initiatives. It does not show how the selected employees experienced a particular programme. The present survey addresses this narrower question through respondents’ accounts of their most recent hybrid-training experience.

The study does not claim that these gaps prove there is no relevant research. Rather, it identifies a limited and fragmented evidence base at the intersection of hybrid learning, employee training effectiveness, public-sector banking, and the Union Bank LMS context. The study contributes evidence from a focused employee group and provides a transparent, literature-based instrument for examining the relationship.
