Chapter 1 — Introduction

## 1.1 Background and Context

The banking sector in India is undergoing rapid technological and organizational transformation. Digital banking, automation, artificial intelligence, regulatory change, cybersecurity requirements, and shifting customer expectations have together increased the need for continuous employee learning and skill development, and banks have responded by relying increasingly on technology-enabled training alongside conventional classroom-based instruction (Jain & Jain, 2015). Employees in banking increasingly encounter a combination of classroom programmes, virtual instructor-led sessions, e-learning modules, learning management systems, webinars, and structured on-the-job assessment, rather than classroom-only training delivery.

This shift did not happen gradually. The COVID-19 pandemic forced most Indian banks to move classroom-based training online within a matter of weeks, and while in-person training has since resumed at many institutions, banks have generally not reverted fully to pre-pandemic, classroom-only delivery. The blended arrangements adopted out of necessity during the pandemic have, in many cases, been retained deliberately once their practical advantages, particularly reduced travel and scheduling pressure across a geographically dispersed branch network, became apparent to training administrators. This makes the present moment a reasonable point at which to examine whether hybrid training, now continued as a matter of ongoing policy rather than emergency response, is achieving its intended effect.

Traditional face-to-face training offers direct interaction, instructor guidance, demonstration, and immediate clarification, while digital learning offers flexibility, accessibility, self-pacing, and scalability that classroom formats cannot easily match. Hybrid or blended learning combines both approaches with the intention of realizing the advantages of each rather than accepting the limitations of either alone. Combining online and classroom components does not, by itself, guarantee that this intention is met. Whether a hybrid programme actually improves learning outcomes depends on how learners engage with the material provided, their readiness to use digital tools, the competence of the trainers delivering the classroom components, the quality of the content itself, and the extent to which the organization supports employees in applying what they have learned.

Evidence from the financial services sector suggests that hybrid formats can produce measurable results when these surrounding factors are in place. Agarwal, Goyal, Kumar and Modi (2026), in a longitudinal study of a hybrid employee-development intervention that combined instructor-led sessions, e-learning, a gamified simulation, on-the-job training and live projects, found that employees who completed the programme showed significantly greater performance improvement than a comparison group that did not. Their study reports this outcome at the level of the overall intervention and does not attempt to separate out which of its several components, or which conditions, were responsible for the improvement observed.

Blended and hybrid learning in Indian workplaces more generally has begun to attract similar research attention outside banking. Saroj, Sahney and Sekar (2026), studying blended-learning training among 375 employees in an Indian organization, found that acceptance of the blended format, instructor competence, and employees' own metacognitive skills were each associated with stronger learner engagement during training, and that this engagement was in turn associated with greater employee agility on the job, an effect strengthened where supervisors were seen as supportive. Read together with Agarwal et al.'s findings, this suggests that hybrid training arrangements are capable of producing real organizational benefit in an Indian setting, but that the benefit runs through the people and conditions surrounding the format rather than from the format itself.

Union Bank of India, the organization studied in this dissertation, already operates within this broader shift toward mixed-delivery training. As Section 1.5 sets out in more detail, the bank runs a residential staff college, a network of regional learning centres, and a mobile e-learning application named Union Vidya, placing it among the public-sector banks that have moved beyond purely classroom-based training well before this study began. This existing infrastructure is what makes Union Bank of India a workable and relevant setting in which to examine hybrid learning, rather than a setting where hybrid delivery would first have to be introduced.

For human resource practice specifically, this question matters beyond any single organization. Training budgets in large banks are substantial, and a decision to continue investing in blended delivery, or to scale back toward one mode or the other, is easier to justify when it rests on an understanding of which elements of a hybrid programme are doing the work, rather than on an assumption that combining formats is inherently sufficient. Studying the mechanism through which hybrid learning affects training outcomes is therefore of practical interest to HR and learning-and-development functions in banking, and not only of academic interest.

This study accordingly treats hybrid learning not merely as a training delivery method to be compared against classroom-only training, but as a learning system in which digital and face-to-face components interact with learner engagement, trainer competence, and organizational context to shape training effectiveness.

## 1.2 Key Concepts and Definitions

**Learning** is understood here as a relatively lasting change in an employee's knowledge, skills, or capacity to apply what has been learned, resulting from experience or instruction. **Training** refers specifically to learning that is deliberately organized by an organization to improve employees' knowledge, skills, or job-related behaviour for a defined purpose.

**Digital learning** refers to learning delivered or supported through digital technologies — online courses, learning-management-system modules, recorded video, mobile learning, and virtual classrooms — offering flexibility and scalability at some cost to face-to-face interaction. **In-person learning** refers to traditional classroom-based instruction involving direct interaction between trainer and trainees, offering immediate feedback and social learning at the cost of scheduling and location flexibility.

**Hybrid or blended learning**, following Garrison and Kanuka (2004), is defined not simply as the co-occurrence of digital and classroom instruction but as their thoughtful integration, such that each mode is deliberately sequenced to reinforce the other rather than duplicating content.

**Training effectiveness** refers to the extent to which training achieves its intended learning and work-related outcomes, commonly assessed across the successive levels of reaction, learning, behaviour, and results identified by Kirkpatrick (1994). This study extends the concept along that same logic, treating learning, retention, and job performance as successive rather than interchangeable outcomes of training.

**Learner engagement**, following Schaufeli, Bakker and Salanova (2006), is understood as a relatively persistent state characterized by vigour, dedication, and absorption, rather than a one-off reaction to a single training session. Within hybrid learning specifically, engagement functions as the mechanism through which programme design translates into effectiveness: an employee who engages more fully with the material, whether it is delivered online or in person, is better positioned to retain and later apply what has been learned.

**Trainer competence** refers to a trainer's ability to deliver content effectively, use classroom and digital tools appropriately, and structure a session in a way that sustains trainee attention and participation. Saroj, Sahney and Sekar (2026), whose model this study extends, treat instructor competence as one of three antecedents of learner engagement in a blended workplace-training setting, alongside acceptance of the blended format and trainees' own metacognitive skill. This study adopts the same conceptual placement, treating trainer competence as a determinant of engagement rather than of training effectiveness directly.

**Knowledge retention** refers to the extent to which an employee continues to hold and can recall training content once the training event itself has ended, as distinct from performance on an immediate post-training test. Wakekar et al. (2026), comparing blended, traditional, and e-learning-only training among community health workers, measured retention at a six-month interval and found it markedly higher in the blended group than in either single-mode group. This supports treating retention as an outcome worth measuring separately, rather than assuming it is identical to training effectiveness or to immediate learning.

**Job performance**, following the in-role/extra-role distinction established by Williams and Anderson (1991), is used here to mean an employee's task-related performance, that is, how effectively core job duties are carried out, rather than the broader category of organizational citizenship behaviour. Koopmans et al. (2011/2014) further separate task performance from contextual performance and counterproductive work behaviour within this wider construct. This study follows their task-performance framing, since it is task-related performance, and not organizational citizenship as such, that hybrid training is most plausibly positioned to influence.

## 1.3 Need, Scope and Significance of the Study

Indian public-sector banks invest substantial resources in employee training to build technical, managerial, digital, and behavioural competencies, and Union Bank of India in particular already operates a formal, mixed-delivery training infrastructure combining a residential staff college, a regionally distributed network of learning centres, and a mobile e-learning platform (see Section 1.5). Understanding whether this infrastructure functions as an effective hybrid learning system, and through what mechanism, is therefore a live organizational question, not only an academic one.

The literature reviewed in Chapter 2 establishes that blended learning is generally associated with positive training outcomes, but leaves four specific gaps unaddressed: an explanatory gap, in that few studies examine the psychological mechanisms through which hybrid learning produces its effects rather than simply whether it does (Ashraf et al., 2021); a population gap, in that the literature is concentrated in higher education rather than organizational training (Bin Mubayrik, 2018); a geographic and sectoral gap, in that very little research addresses hybrid learning specifically within Indian public-sector banking (Jain & Jain, 2015); and a chain-depth gap, in that no identified study tests a complete sequence from hybrid learning through engagement, effectiveness, retention, and job performance within a single model (Saroj, Sahney & Sekar, 2026). This study addresses these four gaps concurrently; Chapter 2 develops each of them in full.

The scope of this study is deliberately bounded. It examines employees of a single organization, Union Bank of India, rather than the banking sector as a whole, and models hybrid learning through six constructs: Hybrid Learning and Trainer Competence as antecedents of Learner Engagement, which is examined as a mediator of Training Effectiveness, which in turn precedes Knowledge Retention and Job Performance as sequential outcomes. Organizational and supervisor support, although identified in the literature as a plausible additional factor, is deliberately excluded from the tested model on the grounds that the sample size available for this study, approximately 80 respondents, is unlikely to support reliable estimation of a more complex model; it is named instead as a direction for future research in Chapter 5. The study is cross-sectional rather than longitudinal, and Knowledge Retention and Job Performance are measured through employees' self-reported perceptions rather than through objective recall tests or supervisor ratings, a scope decision made because administering an objective, delayed knowledge test across a live banking workforce was not practicable within the available data-collection window.

The study's significance is both academic and organizational. Academically, it extends the model tested by Saroj, Sahney and Sekar (2026) beyond employee agility into a fuller, Kirkpatrick-aligned outcome chain, and contributes one of very few studies of hybrid learning conducted specifically within Indian public-sector banking. Organizationally, its findings are intended to inform Union Bank of India's own Learning and Development function: understanding which elements of a hybrid training design most strongly predict engagement, retention, and job performance offers a more defensible basis for future investment in the bank's training infrastructure than continuing to expand hybrid delivery on the assumption that combining formats is inherently beneficial. Since Union Bank of India's own training model, a residential staff college combined with regional learning centres and a mobile e-learning application, broadly resembles the mixed-delivery approach adopted by several other Indian public-sector banks, the findings may be cautiously informative beyond this one organization, although the convenience sample means any such generalization should be treated as suggestive rather than established.

## 1.4 Statement of the Problem

Indian public-sector banks invest substantial resources in employee training to develop technical, managerial, digital, and behavioural competencies, and increasingly deliver this training through a mix of digital and face-to-face formats rather than through classroom instruction alone. This shift raises a straightforward but, as Chapter 2 establishes, so far inadequately answered question: does combining digital and face-to-face training actually improve training effectiveness, and, if so, through what mechanism? Hence, the purpose of this study is to examine not merely whether a hybrid learning model outperforms training delivered through a single mode, but the specific pathway through which it does so.

A hybrid programme may provide flexibility and accessibility yet still fail to achieve its intended outcomes if employees are insufficiently engaged with the material provided, if trainers are unable to deliver classroom and digital content with equal competence, or if the resulting learning is not retained long enough to be applied on the job. This study accordingly frames the problem as a sequential one: Hybrid Learning and Trainer Competence are treated as inputs that shape Learner Engagement, which is examined as the mechanism through which engagement translates into Training Effectiveness, which in turn is expected to shape Knowledge Retention and, ultimately, Job Performance.

No verified study was located that tests this sequence, or a comparable one, specifically within Union Bank of India, despite the bank's own mixed-delivery training infrastructure described in Section 1.5. The problem addressed by this study is therefore stated as follows: does a well-designed hybrid learning model, mediated by learner engagement, improve knowledge retention and job performance among employees of Union Bank of India compared with training delivered predominantly through a single mode, and through what specific mechanism does this occur?

## 1.5 Industry Profile and Company Profile

### Industry Profile: Indian Public-Sector Banking

India's public-sector banking industry took its modern shape through two waves of nationalization, in 1969 and 1980, which brought the majority of Indian banking under government ownership. Union Bank of India was among the banks nationalized in 1969. More recently, the Government of India consolidated ten public-sector banks into four through an amalgamation programme effective 1 April 2020, under which Andhra Bank and Corporation Bank were merged into Union Bank of India. The sector today is undergoing a technology-driven transformation encompassing core banking systems, mobile and internet banking, and centralized customer-relationship platforms, a shift that extends into employee training, which is increasingly delivered through learning-management systems, mobile learning applications, and blended classroom-digital formats.

### Company Profile: Union Bank of India

Union Bank of India was registered as a limited company on 11 November 1919 by Seth Sitaram Poddar, with its head office in Mumbai; the head office building was inaugurated by Mahatma Gandhi in 1921. The bank was nationalized in 1969, and following the 2020 amalgamation with Andhra Bank and Corporation Bank became the fifth-largest public-sector bank in India by scale. This merger is directly relevant to the present study: integrating a workforce drawn from three previously separate organizations into a single set of systems, processes, and organizational culture represents precisely the kind of large-scale reskilling challenge in which the effectiveness of training delivery carries material organizational consequences.

The bank operates approximately 8,650-8,700 domestic branches and reports an employee count in the region of 73,900-74,100 as of the financial year ending 31 March 2025, figures reported consistently across the bank's own disclosures and its most recent annual report; a materially lower figure appearing in some secondary aggregator sources could not be reconciled with these primary figures and should be treated with caution pending direct confirmation. The bank's total business, combining deposits and advances, exceeded Rs. 23.8 lakh crore as of mid-2026, and total assets were reported at approximately ₹14.9 lakh crore for the 2025 financial year. Union Bank of India operates across every Indian state, maintains overseas branches in Dubai and Sydney together with a United Kingdom banking subsidiary, Union Bank of India (UK) Limited, and is listed on both the National Stock Exchange and Bombay Stock Exchange, with the Government of India holding a 74.76 percent equity stake. Its group structure includes domestic subsidiaries in asset management and life insurance, Union Asset Management and Star Union Dai-ichi Life Insurance among them, indicating an organization of sufficient scale and structural complexity that its training function cannot rely on informal or ad hoc arrangements alone.

The bank maintains a dedicated training infrastructure centred on the Union Bank Knowledge Centre, a residential staff college located at Bannerghatta, Bengaluru, supported by a Learning and Development Division at the bank's central office and a network of regional learning centres. The bank's training system has received the Golden Peacock National Training Award on ten occasions, recognition presented by the Institute of Directors as reflecting the strongest training system among Indian public-sector banks. Most directly relevant to the present study, the bank operates a named mobile e-learning platform, Union Vidya, providing employees with training content, interactive scenarios, and progress tracking accessible independently of location; an open tender for learning-management-system subscription, active at the time of writing, indicates continued organizational investment in digital training infrastructure. The bank's broader human-resources transformation initiative, Union Prerna, is explicitly framed in the bank's own communications as combining the "digitalization of processes" with a "learning revolution" within the organization, language that positions the bank's own strategic self-understanding in close alignment with the present study's subject matter. Under this initiative the bank launched two gender-focused committees, EmpowerHer in July 2022 and EmpowerHim the following month, both framed by the bank as part of the same combined digitalization-and-learning programme rather than as a standalone diversity measure.

The bank's investment in technology more broadly has also received recent external recognition: at the Indian Banks' Association's Banking Technology Awards, Union Bank of India was named Best Technology Bank of the Year in 2025, having already won six awards at the same forum's 2024 edition. These are enterprise-technology rather than training-specific awards, but they corroborate that the bank's digital investment, of which its e-learning platform forms one part, is current and active rather than a legacy initiative from an earlier period.

Taken together, this profile indicates that Union Bank of India already operates the specific combination of centralized classroom training, regionally distributed learning centres, and mobile digital learning that constitutes hybrid learning as defined in Section 1.2, making it a substantively appropriate organizational setting in which to examine the research problem stated in Section 1.4.

## 1.6 Chapterization

This dissertation is organized into five chapters.

Chapter 1 introduces the research problem. It covers the background and context of hybrid learning in Indian banking, defines the key concepts used throughout the study, states the need, scope, and significance of the research, formally states the problem, and profiles the banking industry and Union Bank of India, the organization within which the study is conducted.

Chapter 2 reviews the literature relevant to hybrid learning, training effectiveness, and the mechanisms connecting them. It sets out the theoretical framework underlying the study's conceptual model and identifies the four gaps in the existing literature that this study addresses.

Chapter 3 presents the study's methodology. It states the research objectives and hypotheses, defines the variables in the conceptual model, describes the research design and sampling method, explains how data is collected and analysed, and sets out the instrument's pilot testing, reliability, and validity.

Chapter 4 presents the results of the data analysis. It reports descriptive and inferential statistics for the sample, interprets these results against the study's hypotheses, and compares them with the findings of the studies reviewed in Chapter 2.

Chapter 5 concludes the dissertation. It summarizes the study's findings, states its academic and organizational contributions and limitations, and proposes directions for future research; together with Chapters 1 to 4, it also forms the first complete draft of the dissertation submitted at this stage.
